## 1. Can net operating losses be monetized?

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/can-net-operating-losses-be-monetized/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/can-net-operating-losses-be-monetized
- **Category:** M&A and transactions (Corporate taxation · Transactions)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** Net operating losses cannot be sold on their own, but transactions can let a profitable business use them, within the limits of Sections 172, 269, 382, 383 and 384 and the economic substance doctrine.

### The short answer

Not on their own. A net operating loss (NOL) is a tax attribute of the taxpayer that incurred it and cannot be sold or assigned separately. Its value is realized when the loss offsets the taxpayer’s own future taxable income, whether from ordinary profitable operations or through a transaction, most often by bringing a profitable business or income-producing assets into the loss company, or by acquiring the loss company. Such transactions are subject to the limits of Sections 172, 269, 382, 383 and 384 of the Internal Revenue Code and, where it is relevant, the economic substance doctrine, and should make sense apart from their tax benefits.

### What is a net operating loss?

A net operating loss arises when a taxpayer’s allowable deductions exceed its gross income for the year, computed with the statutory adjustments in Section 172(c) and (d). Because of those adjustments, an NOL can differ materially from a loss shown in financial statements. Its treatment depends on when it arose:

- **Losses arising in tax years beginning after 2017** generally carry forward indefinitely and, for tax years beginning after 2020, can offset no more than 80 percent of taxable income (computed before the NOL deduction).
- **Losses arising in 2018, 2019 and 2020** could, under temporary CARES Act rules, be carried back five years, subject to elections to waive the carryback.
- **Losses arising before 2018** generally retain the former rules: a 20-year carryforward and no 80 percent limit.
- **Special rules** apply to farming losses and certain insurance company losses.

A company holding losses from both before and after 2018 deducts the older losses first; the 80 percent limit for the newer losses is then generally calculated on the taxable income remaining after those older deductions. The usable amount in any year therefore depends on the mix.

An NOL is a tax attribute: a right to reduce future taxable income of the taxpayer that holds it, or of a successor that inherits it by statute, such as an acquiring corporation under Section 381, or of an affiliated group under the consolidated return rules, in each case subject to the applicable limits. In financial statements under ASC 740, an NOL carryforward generally gives rise to a deferred tax asset, which is then reduced by a valuation allowance to the extent realization is not more likely than not. The gross asset and its net carrying value can therefore differ substantially.

Holding an NOL is not itself a problem to be solved: a company that returns to profitability realizes the value simply by using the loss against its own income. The transactions discussed below are alternatives for companies that cannot expect to do so on their own.

### Can NOLs be sold on their own?

No. Tax attributes belong to the taxpayer that generated them. A corporation cannot sell or assign its NOLs to another company as a standalone asset. The only way another corporation succeeds to them is by statute: in certain tax-free reorganizations and subsidiary liquidations, Section 381 carries the loss corporation’s attributes over to the acquiring corporation, subject to the limits described below. An individual’s NOL generally cannot be transferred to another person either: losses on a joint return must be allocated between spouses, and a decedent’s unused NOL can generally be used only on the decedent’s final return, including a final joint return with a surviving spouse, after which it expires.

What can change hands is the company that holds the losses, or the income that the losses can shelter. Every NOL transaction is built on one of those two ideas.

> In practice · Aaron Sokol, Principal
>
> Some of the most frequent calls we receive are from individuals with large losses they hope to sell, often after a business that didn’t work out or a hard year in the markets. Most are surprised to learn that a loss, on its own, can’t be sold or handed to someone else. A loss has value only to the taxpayer that owns it, and only when it is used inside a real business with real income. Any transaction built around it has to make economic sense on its own terms, apart from the tax benefit. When those conditions can be met, the path generally follows the structures described in this article. When they can’t, the most valuable advice is often to keep the loss where it is and plan around it.

### How does Section 382 limit NOLs after an ownership change?

Section 382 applies when a loss corporation undergoes an *ownership change*. Under Section 382(g) and the regulations, an ownership change occurs when the aggregate increase in ownership of the corporation’s 5-percent shareholders exceeds 50 percentage points, with each shareholder’s increase measured against the lowest percentage that shareholder owned at any time during the testing period, which is generally three years. This is not the same as a purchase of half the outstanding shares: several smaller increases can add up to an ownership change, and a single large purchase may not be one. Detailed attribution, aggregation, option and public-group rules determine who counts as a 5-percent shareholder and how ownership is measured.

After an ownership change, losses that existed before the change can offset only a limited amount of income each year. The annual limit is generally the value of the loss corporation’s stock immediately before the change multiplied by the long-term tax-exempt rate, which the IRS publishes monthly. That value is adjusted for capital contributions made to inflate it, redemptions and other corporate contractions, and substantial holdings of nonbusiness assets. In the year of the change the limit is prorated, and any unused portion carries forward to later years.

Two further rules often decide the outcome:

- **Continuity of business enterprise.** If the new loss corporation does not continue the old loss corporation’s business enterprise for two years after the change, the annual limit generally becomes zero, so the pre-change losses become effectively unusable, apart from limited increases for certain recognized built-in gains.
- **Built-in gains and losses.** If the company had a net unrealized built-in gain at the time of the change, built-in gains recognized during the five-year recognition period can increase the annual limit. If it had a net unrealized built-in loss, built-in losses recognized during that period are treated as pre-change losses and become subject to the limit themselves. Threshold rules determine whether either regime applies.

Section 383 applies parallel limits to tax credits and capital loss carryforwards.

### What transaction structures may be available?

- **Bringing income to the losses.** A loss company acquires a profitable business or income-producing assets for cash, or for stock that does not cause an ownership change. Section 382 is not triggered by the acquisition itself, but the losses remain subject to any existing Section 382 limit from earlier changes, the 80 percent limit, Section 269, Section 384, consolidated return rules and the economic substance doctrine. Avoiding a new ownership change does not make the losses freely usable.
- **Acquiring the loss company.** A buyer acquires the loss corporation, or merges with it in a tax-free reorganization in which its losses carry over under Section 381. The losses survive, but subject to the Section 382 annual limit and the other rules described here.
- **Recapitalizing the loss company.** New capital is raised in amounts and forms designed to keep cumulative ownership shifts below the 50-point threshold, so the losses avoid a new Section 382 limit, though any existing limits and other rules continue to apply.
- **Restructuring in bankruptcy.** Two special rules apply in a Chapter 11 case. Under Section 382(l)(5), if pre-change shareholders and qualified creditors (generally, holders of debt held for a required period or arising in the ordinary course of business) end up owning at least half of the reorganized company, the ordinary annual limit may not apply; in exchange, losses are reduced by certain interest deductions, and a further ownership change within two years generally causes the Section 382 limitation to become zero. A company may elect out of Section 382(l)(5). Under Section 382(l)(6), the limit still applies, but the company’s value is measured after the restructuring, reflecting the debt cancelled, which usually produces a larger limit.

Public companies with significant NOLs often adopt tax benefit preservation plans, which discourage stock purchases that could trigger an inadvertent ownership change.

### Illustrative example

*Assumptions:* a domestic C corporation holds \$50 million of NOLs arising after 2017; it has no earlier Section 382 limitation and no value adjustments; it earns enough taxable income to use the losses allowed; no later ownership change occurs; the federal rate is a constant 21 percent; and no other rule disallows the losses.

The nominal value of the losses is \$10.5 million of federal tax (\$50 million × 21 percent), an upper bound, not a market or accounting value.

If an investor acquires the company in an ownership change in November 2026, when its equity is worth \$10 million and the IRS’s long-term tax-exempt rate is 4.18 percent, Section 382 limits the use of the losses to \$418,000 a year, worth at most about \$87,800 of federal tax a year. At that pace the losses would take more than a century to use, so their economic value to the buyer is a small fraction of their nominal value.

If the same company instead acquires, without an ownership change, a profitable business that adds \$5 million a year of taxable income before the NOL deduction, it may be able to offset up to 80 percent of that income, or \$4 million a year, saving up to \$840,000 of federal tax annually, subject to Sections 269 and 384, consolidated return rules and the facts.

Illustrative and simplified. Rates change monthly, and the example ignores state tax, timing, the present value of future savings and many other rules that may apply.

### Limitations and risks

- **Section 269.** The IRS can disallow a deduction or credit where the principal purpose of an acquisition of control, or of property with a carryover basis, was the evasion or avoidance of federal income tax by securing a benefit the acquirer would not otherwise enjoy. The rule applies only to acquisitions and benefits within its scope; a tax motive alone does not trigger it, but within that scope the acquisition cannot have tax avoidance as its principal purpose, and avoiding a Section 382 ownership change does not protect a transaction from it.
- **Economic substance.** Where the economic substance doctrine is relevant to a transaction, Section 7701(o) generally requires that it meaningfully change the taxpayer’s economic position apart from federal income tax effects and have a substantial non-federal-income-tax purpose. A transaction that exists only to use a loss can be disregarded, and penalties can apply.
- **Section 384.** When one corporation acquires control of another, or its assets in a reorganization, and either has a net unrealized built-in gain, preacquisition losses of the other generally cannot offset that gain when it is recognized during the five-year recognition period. The rule reaches recognized built-in gains, not ordinary post-acquisition operating income, and its application depends on definitions and exceptions in the statute.
- **Cancellation of debt.** When a distressed company excludes cancellation-of-debt income from tax, for example in bankruptcy or while insolvent, Section 108(b) generally requires it to reduce its tax attributes, starting with NOLs, by the amount excluded. A restructuring can therefore shrink or eliminate the losses it was meant to preserve.
- **Business continuity.** Failing to continue the loss company’s business enterprise for two years after an ownership change generally reduces the Section 382 limit to zero.
- **Measurement.** Whether an ownership change has occurred depends on a detailed study of shareholder history, options and prior transactions. Errors are common and costly.
- **State law.** States set their own NOL rules, and conformity varies. California has suspended NOL deductions for tax years 2024 through 2026 for corporations with \$1 million or more of income subject to California tax and for individuals with \$1 million or more of net business income or modified adjusted gross income. Disaster loss carryovers are not affected, and the carryover period for suspended losses is extended by up to three years. Other states have imposed their own caps or suspensions from time to time.
- **Valuation.** Multiplying a loss by the tax rate gives an upper bound under simplified assumptions, not a market, accounting or realizable economic value. The real value depends on when the loss can actually be used under the applicable limits and on the likelihood of future taxable income.

### What about capital loss carryforwards?

A C corporation’s capital losses can offset only capital gains. Under Section 1212 they generally carry back three years and forward five, then expire; a carryback cannot create or increase an NOL in the earlier year. After an ownership change they are limited under Section 383 alongside NOLs. Because of the short carryforward, timing a transaction that generates capital gains is often the central question.

Individuals are treated differently: net capital losses can offset capital gains plus up to \$3,000 of ordinary income a year (\$1,500 if married filing separately), and the remainder carries forward for life. Married couples filing separately must allocate losses between them under the applicable rules. As with NOLs, an individual’s capital loss carryforward cannot be transferred to another person and expires after the final return following death.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Can an individual sell or transfer an NOL?**

No. An individual’s NOL can be used only on that individual’s own returns, including a final joint return with a surviving spouse, and any unused amount expires after the final return following death.

**Q: Does a merger eliminate a company’s NOLs?**

Not necessarily. In many tax-free reorganizations the losses carry over to the surviving corporation under Section 381, but they become subject to the Section 382 annual limit if an ownership change has occurred, and to the other rules described in this article.

**Q: What is the long-term tax-exempt rate?**

A rate the IRS publishes each month for Section 382 purposes, equal to the highest of the adjusted federal long-term rates for the current month and the two prior months. The rate for the month of the ownership change is used to calculate the annual limit. For November 2026 it is 4.18 percent.

**Q: How much is an NOL worth?**

Its nominal value is the loss multiplied by the tax rate, which is an upper bound. Its economic value depends on how quickly it can be used under the applicable limits and on the likelihood of future taxable income, and is usually much lower.

## 2. Solo 401(k) vs. self-directed IRA, and when Roth makes sense

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/solo-401k-vs-self-directed-ira/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/solo-401k-vs-self-directed-ira
- **Category:** Retirement accounts (Retirement accounts · Tax strategy)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How a solo 401(k) and a self-directed IRA differ on eligibility, contribution limits, investments, loans, leveraged real estate, prohibited transactions and creditor protection, and the trade-offs of the Roth versions of each.

### The short answer

A solo 401(k) is an employer retirement plan for a business whose only participants are its owners (and their spouses), with no other employees required to be covered. It allows much larger contributions than an IRA (up to \$72,000 in 2026 before catch-up contributions, subject to compensation), can permit loans if the plan document allows, and may qualify for a statutory exception from tax on certain debt-financed real estate. A self-directed IRA is available to anyone with eligible compensation (or a spouse with compensation) and can hold similar alternative investments, but contributions are capped at \$7,500 (\$8,600 at age 50 and over) and loans are not allowed. The Roth version of either gives up a current deduction in exchange for tax-free qualified distributions later. Which is better depends on the owner’s business, contribution capacity, investments and administrative tolerance.

### What is a solo 401(k)?

A solo 401(k), also called a one-participant 401(k), is an ordinary 401(k) plan sponsored by a business whose only participants are its owner or owners and their spouses. Sole proprietors, single-member LLCs, partnerships and S or C corporations can sponsor one. The question is not whether the business has any employees at all but whether it has employees the plan would be required to cover. Some workers can be excluded under the eligibility rules, but long-term part-time employees, employees of related businesses in a controlled group or affiliated service group, and partners in a multi-owner partnership change the analysis.

The owner contributes in two capacities:

- **As employee**, the owner can defer up to \$24,500 of compensation in 2026, plus an \$8,000 catch-up at age 50 or over, or \$11,250 at ages 60 through 63. The deferral limit applies per person across all 401(k), 403(b) and similar plans subject to the shared limit of Section 402(g), not per business; governmental 457(b) plans generally have a separate limit.
- **As employer**, the business can add a profit-sharing contribution of up to 25 percent of W-2 compensation. For a sole proprietor or partner, the equivalent is roughly 20 percent of net earnings from self-employment after deducting half of self-employment tax. S corporation distributions are not compensation for this purpose; only W-2 wages count.

Total contributions are capped at \$72,000 for 2026, before catch-up contributions, and cannot exceed the owner’s compensation.

**The 2026 Roth catch-up rule.** Beginning in 2026, a participant whose prior-year Social Security (FICA) wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded \$150,000 must make any catch-up contributions as Roth contributions. This most often affects owners of S corporations who pay themselves W-2 wages. Sole proprietors and partners, who have no FICA wages from the business, are generally outside the wage test.

### What is a self-directed IRA?

A self-directed IRA is a traditional or Roth IRA held with a custodian that permits investments beyond publicly traded securities, such as real estate, private loans, private company shares and LLC interests. The tax rules are the same as for any IRA; what differs is the custodian, which allows the investment and holds title on the account’s behalf.

Contributions are limited to \$7,500 for 2026, plus a \$1,100 catch-up at age 50 or over, in total across all of a person’s traditional and Roth IRAs, and require eligible compensation, although a spouse without compensation can contribute based on a working spouse’s income on a joint return. A deduction for a traditional IRA contribution phases out at higher incomes for people covered by a workplace plan. Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out between \$153,000 and \$168,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and between \$242,000 and \$252,000 for married couples filing jointly.

### Pros and cons of each

#### Solo 401(k)

- **Pros.** Much higher contribution capacity; a Roth option at any income; loans if the plan permits; a possible exception for certain debt-financed real estate; and, with a suitable plan document, trustee-directed investing without a custodian’s charge on each transaction.
- **Cons.** Available only while no other employees must be covered; a plan document and Form 5500-EZ filings; the owner, as trustee, bears fiduciary and administrative responsibility; and creditor protection outside bankruptcy depends on state law.

#### Self-directed IRA

- **Pros.** Available without a business; simple to open; can receive rollovers from employer plans; and can hold most investments other than life insurance and most collectibles.
- **Cons.** Low annual contributions; no loans; tax on income from most leveraged investments; custodian fees; and limited creditor protection in some states. In California, for example, IRAs are generally protected from judgment creditors only to the extent necessary for support.

### Prohibited transactions

Both accounts are subject to the prohibited transaction rules of Section 4975, which also contain statutory and administrative exemptions for certain transactions. Directing the account’s investments is permitted. What is prohibited is dealing between the account and a *disqualified person*, a category that includes the owner and other fiduciaries, the owner’s spouse, ancestors, lineal descendants and their spouses, and entities in which those persons hold a significant interest. Common problems include:

- selling or leasing property between the account and a disqualified person, or lending money in either direction;
- a disqualified person providing services to the account or its property, or being compensated by it, unless an exemption applies;
- personal use of an account asset, such as living in or vacationing at a property the account owns; and
- personally guaranteeing a loan to the account.

The consequences differ by account. If an IRA owner engages in a prohibited transaction, the account generally ceases to be an IRA under Section 408(e)(2) and is treated as distributed at its fair market value on the first day of that year; the amount above any after-tax basis is taxable, and an early-distribution penalty may apply. For a Roth IRA, the deemed distribution follows the Roth ordering rules (regular contributions first, then conversions, then earnings), so only earnings in a nonqualified distribution are generally taxable, although penalties can apply to earnings and to recently converted amounts. In a qualified plan such as a solo 401(k), the result is instead generally an excise tax under Section 4975 on the disqualified person (15 percent, rising to 100 percent if not corrected), with correction required and possible consequences for the plan’s qualified status.

### Roth or traditional?

A traditional contribution may be deductible (for an IRA, depending on income and workplace plan coverage), and distributions are taxed except to the extent they represent after-tax basis. A Roth contribution is made after tax, and *qualified* distributions, including earnings, are tax-free. A Roth distribution is generally qualified only after a five-year holding period and once the owner reaches 59½, dies or becomes disabled, or, for a Roth IRA, for a first-time home purchase up to a \$10,000 lifetime limit.

#### Pros of Roth

- Tax-free qualified distributions, which matter most for assets expected to appreciate sharply.
- No required minimum distributions during the owner’s life, for Roth IRAs and, since 2024, for designated Roth accounts in 401(k) plans.
- Tax diversification: money that can be drawn in retirement without raising taxable income.
- Qualified distributions to heirs are generally income-tax-free. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited account within ten years, with exceptions for eligible designated beneficiaries, and earnings can be taxable if the five-year period was not yet met.

#### Cons of Roth

- No deduction today, which costs most when current tax rates are higher than expected future rates.
- Separate five-year rules apply to qualified distributions and to each conversion, and Roth IRA and Roth 401(k) rules are not identical.
- Direct Roth IRA contributions are barred above the income limits. A backdoor Roth (a nondeductible IRA contribution converted to Roth) is available, but under the pro-rata rule of Section 408(d)(2) the conversion is partly taxable if the owner holds other pre-tax traditional, SEP or SIMPLE IRA balances.
- A conversion is taxable to the extent it exceeds the owner’s after-tax basis.

### Which is right?

For a self-employed owner with no other employees to cover and the income to contribute substantially, a solo 401(k) usually offers more capacity and flexibility. A self-directed IRA suits people without a qualifying business, those consolidating rollovers, and investors who want a simple account for a few alternative holdings. The answer depends on contribution capacity, administrative cost, the investments involved, any financing and the owner’s wider tax position, and many owners use both.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Can I have a solo 401(k) and an IRA at the same time?**

Yes. IRA contributions are limited separately, though participation in a solo 401(k) counts as workplace plan coverage for the traditional IRA deduction phase-out.

**Q: Can my solo 401(k) or IRA buy real estate?**

Yes, if the plan or custodian permits it. The property must be held as an investment, with expenses paid by the account and income returned to it, and no disqualified person may use it or deal with it. Leveraged purchases raise separate unrelated business income questions.

**Q: What happens if I hire employees?**

Once the business has employees the plan must cover, the plan must include them under the usual coverage and nondiscrimination rules, and a solo plan is generally amended or replaced.

**Q: Can rolling IRAs into a solo 401(k) help with a backdoor Roth?**

Often. If the plan accepts rollovers, moving pre-tax IRA balances into it can reduce the pro-rata tax on a later backdoor conversion. After-tax IRA basis generally cannot be rolled into the plan.

## 3. Revocable vs. irrevocable trusts

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trusts/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trusts
- **Category:** Trusts and estate planning (Trusts · Estate planning)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** What each kind of trust does and does not do for probate, control, creditor protection, estate tax and income tax, and why irrevocability alone does not guarantee estate exclusion or protection.

### The short answer

A revocable trust can be changed or cancelled at any time, so the person who creates it keeps full control. It can avoid probate for assets titled in it and provides for incapacity, but it offers no protection from the creator’s creditors and no estate tax savings. An irrevocable trust generally cannot be changed once funded. Irrevocability alone does not produce its benefits: an irrevocable trust may remove assets and future growth from the taxable estate, and may protect them from creditors, depending on the particular interests and powers the creator retains and on applicable tax and state law. Some administrative and investment powers can be retained without defeating the intended treatment; others cannot.

### What is a revocable trust?

A revocable trust, often called a living trust, holds assets during the creator’s lifetime under terms the creator can change at will. The creator is usually the trustee and the beneficiary while alive, names a successor trustee to take over at incapacity or death, and directs how the assets pass afterward.

For tax purposes a revocable trust is a grantor trust: income is reported on the creator’s own return, and the assets are included in the creator’s taxable estate under Section 2038. Because the assets are included, they generally receive a new basis equal to fair market value at death under Section 1014, which may be higher or lower than the creator’s basis. Some assets, such as retirement accounts and other income in respect of a decedent, do not receive this adjustment.

### What is an irrevocable trust?

An irrevocable trust is one the creator cannot simply amend or revoke. The creator transfers assets to a trustee, who manages them for the beneficiaries under the trust’s terms.

Irrevocability is the starting point, not the result. Whether the assets are outside the creator’s estate depends on the specific interests and powers the creator keeps, some of which are compatible with exclusion and some of which are not: a retained right to income or use, certain powers over the trust and some powers of appointment can cause inclusion under Sections 2036, 2038 and 2041. Whether the assets are protected from the creator’s creditors depends on whether the creator is also a beneficiary and on state law.

For income tax, an irrevocable trust may be a grantor trust (with income reported by the creator), partly a grantor trust, or a separate taxpayer. A separate (non-grantor) trust generally deducts distributions to the extent of its distributable net income, which beneficiaries then report according to its character; income retained by the trust is taxed to the trust at compressed rates. Not every distribution is taxable to the beneficiary: amounts in excess of distributable net income and certain specific gifts generally are not.

“Irrevocable” is less rigid than it sounds. Trust protectors, decanting, nonjudicial settlement agreements and powers of appointment can allow terms to adapt, within limits, without returning control to the creator.

### How do they compare?

|                                            | Revocable trust                                                                                                     | Irrevocable trust                                                                                                               |
|--------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Can be changed                             | Yes, at any time                                                                                                    | Generally no, except through built-in flexibility or state-law procedures                                                       |
| Avoids probate                             | For assets validly held by the trust                                                                                | For assets validly held by the trust                                                                                            |
| Protection from the creator’s creditors    | None                                                                                                                | Possible when the creator is not a beneficiary, or under a state DAPT statute; generally not for a self-settled trust otherwise |
| Excluded from the creator’s taxable estate | No                                                                                                                  | Possible if the creator retains no interests or powers that cause inclusion                                                     |
| Basis adjustment at death                  | Generally yes (up or down), except income in respect of a decedent                                                  | Generally no, unless the assets are included in the estate                                                                      |
| Income tax                                 | Reported by the creator                                                                                             | Grantor trust, partial grantor trust, or separate taxpayer with distributions taxed to beneficiaries                            |
| Privacy                                    | Generally more private than probate, but not confidential in litigation or to beneficiaries entitled to information | Same                                                                                                                            |

### Pros and cons of a revocable trust

- **Pros.** Avoids the cost, delay and publicity of probate for assets titled in the trust, which can be considerable in states such as California; provides continuity if the creator becomes incapacitated; preserves the basis adjustment at death; and is easy to change.
- **Cons.** No protection from the creator’s creditors (California Probate Code section 18200 makes revocable trust property subject to them); no estate tax savings; and it controls only assets actually retitled into it or passing to it by other arrangements.

### Pros and cons of an irrevocable trust

- **Pros.** When properly designed and funded, can remove assets and their future growth from the taxable estate; can protect assets for beneficiaries from their creditors through spendthrift and discretionary terms; can hold life insurance outside the estate; and can last for several generations in states that permit long-duration trusts.
- **Cons.** Loss of direct control and access; assets outside the estate generally do not receive a basis adjustment at death (Revenue Ruling 2023-2 confirms that grantor trust status alone does not produce one when the assets are not acquired from the decedent); retained income in a non-grantor trust is taxed at compressed rates; and setup and trustee costs.

### Creditor protection: whose creditors?

The distinction that matters is between a trust created for others and a trust the creator can benefit from.

- **Trusts for others.** A beneficiary’s interest in a trust created by someone else, especially a discretionary interest protected by a spendthrift clause, is generally shielded from the beneficiary’s creditors, subject to state-law exceptions such as certain support claims.
- **Self-settled trusts.** When the creator is also a beneficiary, most states let the creator’s creditors reach the trust. In California, Probate Code section 15304 makes a spendthrift restraint invalid against the creator’s creditors and lets them reach the maximum amount the trustee could pay the creator, up to the creator’s contribution.
- **Domestic asset protection trusts.** Some states permit self-settled trusts that resist the creator’s future creditors under statutory conditions. See [five commonly considered DAPT states](/knowledge/best-domestic-asset-protection-trust-states).

Trust assets are also not automatically beyond the reach of a beneficiary’s divorcing spouse; the result depends on state marital property law, the beneficiary’s rights and the trustee’s discretion.

### Common types of irrevocable trust

- **Irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT).** Owns life insurance so the death benefit can be excluded from the insured’s estate, provided the insured holds no incidents of ownership under Section 2042. If the insured transfers an existing policy to the trust, the proceeds are generally included if the insured dies within three years under Section 2035, subject to its conditions and exceptions, including transfers for full consideration in a bona fide sale. Having the trust buy a new policy avoids the issue.
- **Spousal lifetime access trust (SLAT).** An irrevocable trust created and funded by one spouse for the benefit of the other spouse and possibly other beneficiaries. The donor spouse may benefit indirectly through the beneficiary spouse; what happens to that indirect benefit on divorce or the beneficiary spouse’s death depends on the trust instrument and applicable law. Spouses who create similar trusts for each other risk having them disregarded under the reciprocal trust doctrine.
- **Intentionally defective grantor trust (IDGT).** Designed to be outside the estate for estate tax purposes but a grantor trust for income tax purposes, often used for estate freezes.
- **Dynasty trust.** Holds assets for multiple generations. Whether it avoids estate and generation-skipping transfer tax at each generation depends on allocating GST exemption, the beneficiaries’ powers and the state’s perpetuities rules.
- **Domestic asset protection trust (DAPT).** A self-settled trust under a state statute, of which the creator can remain a discretionary beneficiary.
- **Charitable remainder and lead trusts.** Split interests between family and charity. See [charitable giving strategies](/knowledge/charitable-giving-strategies).

### Why the estate tax exemption matters

For 2026 the federal estate and gift tax exemption is \$15 million per person, adjusted for inflation in later years and reduced by taxable gifts the person has already made. A married couple can shelter up to \$30 million by using both spouses’ exemptions, including through portability, which generally requires a timely estate tax return election at the first death. The generation-skipping transfer tax exemption is not portable. Families below these levels often rely mainly on revocable trusts and value the basis adjustment at death; families above them, or with fast-appreciating assets or significant liability exposure, typically add irrevocable trusts. Several states impose their own estate taxes at much lower thresholds.

### Using both

Most comprehensive plans use both. A revocable trust serves as the hub that receives assets at death and avoids probate, while one or more irrevocable trusts hold the assets intended for estate tax savings, protection or long-term family stewardship.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Does a revocable trust protect assets from lawsuits?**

No. Because the creator can revoke it and take the assets back, the creator’s creditors can reach them during the creator’s lifetime.

**Q: Does an irrevocable trust automatically protect assets or avoid estate tax?**

No. Both depend on design. A trust the creator can benefit from is generally reachable by the creator’s creditors outside DAPT states, and retained interests or powers can cause estate inclusion.

**Q: Can an irrevocable trust ever be changed?**

Often, within limits. Depending on the trust terms and state law, a trust protector, decanting, nonjudicial settlement agreements or court modification can change administrative and sometimes dispositive terms.

**Q: Do assets in an irrevocable trust get a basis adjustment at death?**

Generally not, unless the assets are included in the creator’s estate. Some trusts give an independent party a power, for example to grant the creator a testamentary general power of appointment, that can cause inclusion when the basis adjustment would be worth more than the estate tax cost. Such powers have their own estate, gift and generation-skipping tax consequences and must be drafted and exercised carefully.

## 4. Five commonly considered asset protection trust states compared

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/best-domestic-asset-protection-trust-states/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/best-domestic-asset-protection-trust-states
- **Category:** Asset protection (Asset protection · Trust jurisdictions)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** Nevada, South Dakota, Delaware, Alaska and Wyoming compared on limitation periods for existing and future creditors, exception creditors, state income tax and trust duration, with the limits every domestic asset protection trust shares.

### The short answer

A domestic asset protection trust (DAPT) is an irrevocable trust, permitted by statute in about twenty states, that the creator can benefit from while limiting the creator’s creditors’ access to its assets after a statutory limitation period. Nevada, South Dakota, Delaware, Alaska and Wyoming are among the states most commonly considered. Nevada, South Dakota and Wyoming use two-year periods for many claims, while Delaware and Alaska use four; each state treats existing and future creditors differently, and each except Nevada names categories of exception creditors who can reach the trust. Many practitioners regard Nevada and South Dakota as the strongest, but that is a professional assessment rather than a legal conclusion. Transfers made when a claim exists or is foreseeable carry heightened risk, and protection for residents of states without a DAPT statute is uncertain.

### What is a domestic asset protection trust?

Traditionally, a person could not create a trust for their own benefit and keep it away from their own creditors. In 1997 Alaska changed that rule, and Delaware followed the same year. Today roughly twenty states permit a self-settled spendthrift trust, commonly called a domestic asset protection trust or DAPT.

The creator transfers assets to an irrevocable trust with at least one qualified trustee in the DAPT state and remains eligible for discretionary distributions. Each statute sets limitation periods within which a creditor can challenge a transfer as fraudulent (voidable); those periods are generally different for creditors whose claims existed at the time of the transfer and for later creditors. Once the applicable period has run, creditors generally cannot reach the trust property, except for any categories of “exception creditors” the state allows.

### What to compare

- **Limitation periods** for existing and for future creditors, including any period that runs from the creditor’s discovery of the transfer.
- **Exception creditors:** claimants, such as certain support or marital claimants or preexisting tort claimants, who can reach the trust regardless.
- **State income tax** on trust income, which depends on the residence of the creator, trustees and beneficiaries, where the trust is administered and the source of its income.
- **Trust duration** and **privacy** of court proceedings.

### Nevada

**Features.** Two-year periods for many claims; a statute that names no exception creditors, which the Nevada Supreme Court applied to spousal and child support claims in *Klabacka v. Nelson* (2017), although transfers can still be challenged within the limitation periods; no state personal income tax; trusts lasting up to 365 years; and charging order protection for LLCs.

**Considerations.** Courts in other states may be less willing to apply Nevada law for a non-resident creator, and the absence of exception creditors may draw scrutiny in family law matters.

### South Dakota

**Features.** Two-year periods for many claims; no rule against perpetuities; no state personal income tax; statutory sealing of certain trust court proceedings; and developed directed trust and trust protector law.

**Considerations.** Exception creditors for certain support and marital claims.

### Delaware

**Features.** A long-established trust statute, the Court of Chancery, flexible directed trust law, and generally no Delaware income tax on trust income accumulated for beneficiaries who are not Delaware residents.

**Considerations.** Four-year periods and exception creditors that include certain marital and preexisting tort claims.

### Alaska

**Features.** The first DAPT statute (1997), no state personal income tax and long-duration trusts.

**Considerations.** Four-year periods, and *Toni 1 Trust v. Wacker* (2018), in which the Alaska Supreme Court held that Alaska’s statute cannot prevent other states’ courts from hearing fraudulent transfer claims involving Alaska trusts.

### Wyoming

**Features.** Two-year periods for many claims following a 2021 amendment; no state personal income tax; trusts lasting up to 1,000 years; and LLC charging order statutes that are often used alongside a trust.

**Considerations.** Fewer decided cases testing its trust statute.

### Limits every DAPT shares

- **Residence of the creator.** A creator who lives in a state without a DAPT statute, such as California, faces the risk that a home-state court applies its own law. In *In re Huber* (2013), a bankruptcy court set aside transfers by a Washington resident to an Alaska trust.
- **Bankruptcy.** Section 548(e) of the Bankruptcy Code permits a bankruptcy trustee to avoid a transfer of the debtor’s property, made within ten years before the filing to a self-settled trust or similar device of which the debtor is a beneficiary, if the debtor made it with actual intent to hinder, delay or defraud a present or future creditor. Avoidance is not automatic; each element must be proved.
- **Interstate enforcement.** A money judgment from one state can generally be enforced in another, and courts disagree about whether a creditor’s home-state fraudulent transfer law can be applied to a DAPT. Assets located in the creator’s home state are the most exposed.
- **Timing.** Transfers made when a claim exists or is reasonably foreseeable carry heightened risk: existing creditors generally have longer, discovery-based periods; fraudulent transfer remedies and bankruptcy avoidance remain available; and courts may use their equitable and contempt powers.
- **Estate tax.** A DAPT can be designed as a completed gift, but completed-gift status does not by itself keep the assets out of the creator’s estate; retained interests, implied understandings about distributions, and the creditor-access rules of the governing state all bear on estate inclusion.

For residents of states without a DAPT statute, an offshore trust or a hybrid design is sometimes considered. See [offshore asset protection jurisdictions compared](/knowledge/offshore-asset-protection-trust-jurisdictions).

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Do I have to live in Nevada or South Dakota to use its DAPT?**

No. Non-residents can create one by using a qualified trustee in that state and keeping some administration there, but protection is generally strongest when the creator, the trustee and the assets all have ties to the DAPT state.

**Q: Can I be the trustee of my own DAPT?**

Not as the required qualified trustee. The statutes require at least one trustee in the state, such as a trust company or resident individual, and limit the powers the creator may hold. Depending on the state, the creator may be able to retain an investment role, a veto over distributions or the power to replace trustees, but not unrestricted control.

**Q: Is a DAPT a grantor trust for income tax?**

Usually yes, so the creator reports the income. For gift and estate tax it can be designed as a completed or incomplete gift, with different consequences; a completed gift does not automatically exclude the assets from the estate.

**Q: Which state is best?**

Many practitioners favor Nevada or South Dakota when creditor protection is the priority, but the answer depends on residence, the assets involved, family circumstances, tax considerations and whether estate planning is also a goal.

## 5. Offshore asset protection trusts: the Cook Islands, Nevis and other jurisdictions compared

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/offshore-asset-protection-trust-jurisdictions/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/offshore-asset-protection-trust-jurisdictions
- **Category:** Offshore planning (Asset protection · Offshore jurisdictions)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How the Cook Islands and Nevis trust statutes treat creditor challenges, how other jurisdictions differ, the limits U.S. courts impose on offshore structures, and the U.S. reporting that applies to U.S. persons involved with foreign trusts.

### The short answer

Offshore trusts are recognized legal structures used for estate planning and wealth management, and some jurisdictions, notably the Cook Islands and Nevis, have trust statutes designed to limit creditor challenges. Under those statutes a creditor generally must bring a claim in the local court within short limitation periods and prove fraudulent intent beyond a reasonable doubt, and the statutes limit the effect of foreign judgments on trust property; Nevis also requires a creditor to post a bond before suing. These features concern the trust’s property, not the settlor personally: U.S. courts retain jurisdiction over U.S. persons and assets located in the United States, and settlors have been held in contempt. U.S. persons who create, fund or receive distributions from a foreign trust generally have federal tax reporting obligations that depend on the facts.

The Cook Islands statute is linked below. The Nevis International Exempt Trust Ordinance and the other jurisdictions’ laws are described from secondary sources and should be verified, in their current consolidated form, by counsel admitted in each jurisdiction before any reliance.

### Why do families consider an offshore trust?

A domestic asset protection trust depends on U.S. courts applying another state’s law. An offshore trust instead places the trustee and the governing law outside the United States. The Cook Islands and Nevis statutes require a creditor who wants to reach trust property to bring a new claim in the local court, under local rules, and limit the effect of foreign judgments against that property. For those reasons offshore trusts are often considered by U.S. families seeking long-term protection, including residents of states such as California that do not recognize self-settled trusts. Their effectiveness depends on the jurisdiction, the timing of funding, where the assets are located, the independence of the trustee and the conduct of the settlor.

### How are these trusts typically structured?

The settlor establishes an irrevocable trust with a trustee licensed in the chosen jurisdiction and is usually one of several discretionary beneficiaries. The trust may own an entity that holds investments. The trustee’s independence is central: a trust whose assets the settlor can in practice direct offers little protection and, as discussed below, can expose the settlor to contempt. Trust instruments may include a protector with defined powers. None of these provisions relieves anyone of the obligation to comply with an enforceable order of a court with jurisdiction over them.

### The Cook Islands and Nevis compared

|                              | Cook Islands                                                                                                                                                                                             | Nevis                                                                                                |
|------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Statute                      | International Trusts Act 1984                                                                                                                                                                            | Nevis International Exempt Trust Ordinance (1994, as amended)                                        |
| What the creditor must prove | Beyond a reasonable doubt, that the transfer was made with principal intent to defraud that creditor and rendered the settlor insolvent or without property to satisfy the claim                         | Beyond a reasonable doubt, intent to defraud, on elements closely modeled on the Cook Islands Act    |

Simplified summary. Each statute contains detailed elements, exceptions and procedures, and the application of its time limits depends on when the claim arose relative to the transfer.

### Other jurisdictions

Several other financial centers have trust laws that are sometimes discussed in this context but that differ materially from the Cook Islands and Nevis model. They are noted here for orientation rather than compared in detail:

- **Belize.** Its Trusts Act addresses foreign judgments and creditor claims in terms that promoters sometimes describe as “immediate protection”; that description overstates what has been tested in contested cases.
- **Cayman Islands.** A major financial center with sophisticated trust law, including STAR trusts. Its Fraudulent Dispositions Act allows certain transfers made at an undervalue with intent to defraud to be set aside within six years, and its trust law is not designed around self-settled creditor protection.
- **Bahamas, Gibraltar, Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man.** Established trust jurisdictions used principally for multigenerational trusts, administration and family wealth structures; their creditor-challenge rules differ from the Cook Islands and Nevis model and should be reviewed with local counsel.
- **Liechtenstein** has its own trust and foundation law. **Switzerland** has no domestic trust law; it recognizes foreign trusts under the Hague Trusts Convention, so Swiss trustees administer trusts governed by other jurisdictions’ laws.

### U.S. tax and reporting

An offshore trust is not a tax shelter. A foreign trust with a U.S. settlor and U.S. beneficiaries is generally treated as a grantor trust under Section 679, so its income is taxed to the settlor. Reporting obligations depend on U.S. ownership, transfers, distributions and account values, and commonly include Form 3520 (transfers to and distributions from foreign trusts), Form 3520-A (the trust’s annual information return), the FBAR for foreign financial accounts and Form 8938 where its thresholds are met. Section 6677 imposes substantial penalties for failures to file foreign trust information, generally the greater of \$10,000 or a percentage of the amount involved.

### Limits and risks

- **U.S. court authority.** U.S. courts retain jurisdiction over U.S. persons and assets located in the United States, and can order a settlor to repatriate assets. In *FTC v. Affordable Media* (9th Cir. 1999) and *In re Lawrence* (11th Cir. 2002), courts rejected settlors’ claims that their trusts made compliance impossible and held them in civil contempt, treating an inability the settlor created as no defense.
- **U.S.-located assets.** Real estate and accounts in the United States remain subject to U.S. courts regardless of the trust’s governing law.
- **Timing.** A transfer made when a claim exists or is foreseeable can be avoided as a voidable transfer under U.S. law, and in bankruptcy Section 548(e) permits avoidance of certain transfers to self-settled trusts made within ten years with actual intent to hinder, delay or defraud creditors.
- **Cost and administration.** Trustee, legal and reporting costs are significant and continuing.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Are offshore trusts legal?**

Offshore trusts are recognized legal structures that may serve legitimate estate planning and wealth management purposes. Their formation, funding, administration and tax treatment must comply with applicable U.S. and foreign law. They do not eliminate existing legal obligations or the authority of courts with jurisdiction over relevant persons and assets.

**Q: Do the assets have to leave the United States?**

Not necessarily, but assets that remain in the United States remain subject to U.S. courts.

**Q: How do the Cook Islands and Nevis differ?**

Their statutes are similar in design. The Cook Islands has the longer history and more reported decisions; Nevis adds a requirement that a creditor post a bond before suing. The choice should be made with counsel in each jurisdiction.

**Q: Can an offshore trust protect U.S. real estate?**

Generally not on its own. Real estate remains subject to the courts where it is located. See [equity stripping](/knowledge/equity-stripping) for how financing affects the equity in real property.

## 6. Equity stripping: how it works and where it fails

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/equity-stripping/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/equity-stripping
- **Category:** Asset protection (Asset protection · Real estate)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How encumbering an asset with genuine, documented debt affects what a judgment creditor can reach, the legal limits under voidable transfer and bankruptcy law, and why the borrowed funds remain the borrower’s assets.

### The short answer

Equity stripping refers to placing genuine, documented debt against an asset, usually real estate or a business, so that less equity remains for a later judgment creditor to reach after secured lenders are paid. It is lawful only when the debt is real (funds actually advanced on commercial terms and serviced) and the transaction is not a voidable transfer, which depends on the borrower’s intent, the value received and the borrower’s solvency. A recorded lien does not automatically outrank every claim, related-party loans receive close scrutiny, and the borrowed funds remain the borrower’s assets with their own creditor, tax and solvency consequences.

### What is equity stripping?

A judgment creditor collects by levying on a debtor’s non-exempt assets. Valid liens that have priority over the judgment creditor are paid first from a sale, so the creditor generally reaches only the equity that remains. Equity stripping is the practice of reducing that equity by borrowing against the asset.

A property worth \$2 million with no debt has \$2 million of equity. The same property with \$1.6 million of valid, senior secured debt has \$400,000, less the costs of a forced sale. The protection, to the extent there is any, comes from a lender’s genuine prior claim, not from hiding the asset.

### Common forms

- **Financing from an unrelated lender.** A conventional mortgage, line of credit or business loan from an independent bank or private lender. Genuine third-party financing is generally less exposed to recharacterization than related-party lending, but it remains subject to the voidable transfer, preference and other avoidance rules described below.
- **Affiliated or related-party lending.** A loan from an entity connected to the borrower or the borrower’s family. Commercial terms are necessary but do not by themselves protect such loans; courts examine whether value was actually exchanged, whether the debt is enforced, and the borrower’s intent and solvency, and may recharacterize or avoid the obligation.
- **Cross-collateralization.** Several properties or entities pledge assets for one another’s genuine obligations. This also exposes each pledged asset to enforcement if any of the covered obligations defaults, increasing the owner’s overall financial risk.
- **Business assets.** Receivables, equipment and intellectual property can be pledged under UCC Article 9. A security interest must first *attach* (value given, rights in the collateral and an authenticated security agreement), then be *perfected*, usually by filing a financing statement; priority among competing claims is then determined by Article 9’s rules. A filed financing statement alone does not create a security interest.

### Does a recorded lien always come first?

No. Priority depends on timing, recording and perfection, and on statutes that give certain claims special status. Earlier recorded liens, some tax liens, mechanics’ liens and other statutory liens can rank ahead of or alongside a new loan, and a lien that is avoided as a voidable transfer or preference provides no priority at all.

### What happens to the borrowed money?

Borrowing converts equity in the property into cash or other assets, which remain the borrower’s property. Whether those assets can be reached by creditors depends on how and when they are held and on applicable exemption, trust and voidable transfer law, and moving them can itself be challenged. Any plan for the proceeds should be reviewed by counsel for creditor-law, tax and solvency consequences.

**Interest deductibility.** The use of the proceeds also determines the tax treatment of the interest. Under the tracing rules of Treasury Regulation 1.163-8T, interest is generally allocated according to how the borrowed funds are spent, not which property secures the loan: proceeds used in a trade or business generally produce business interest (subject to the Section 163(j) limitation), proceeds used to buy investments produce investment interest (deductible only up to net investment income), proceeds used for personal purposes produce generally nondeductible personal interest, and a loan secured by a residence produces deductible qualified residence interest only to the extent the funds acquire or substantially improve that residence, within the dollar limits of Section 163(h). Borrowing against property for asset protection reasons therefore does not by itself make the interest deductible.

### The legal limits

- **Actual intent.** Under the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act, adopted in California (Civil Code section 3439.04(a)(1)) and most states, a transfer made or obligation incurred with actual intent to hinder, delay or defraud any creditor can be avoided. Receiving reasonably equivalent value does not by itself defeat such a claim, although a transferee or obligee who took in good faith and for reasonably equivalent value has defenses under Civil Code section 3439.08.
- **Constructive fraud.** Without proof of intent, a transfer or obligation can still be avoided if the debtor did not receive reasonably equivalent value and either was left with unreasonably small assets for its business, intended or reasonably should have believed it would incur debts beyond its ability to pay (section 3439.04(a)(2), available to present and future creditors), or was insolvent or became insolvent as a result (section 3439.05, available to creditors whose claims arose before the transfer).
- **Bankruptcy.** A bankruptcy trustee can avoid fraudulent transfers and obligations under Section 548, use state voidable transfer law through Section 544, and avoid as preferences under Section 547 certain transfers to existing creditors made shortly before bankruptcy, which can include payments and the grant of a security interest for an earlier debt.
- **Sham debt.** An obligation with no funds advanced, no payments and no intention of enforcement can be disregarded, and recording one after a claim arises invites both avoidance and further liability.
- **Timing.** Financing arranged after a claim has arisen or is reasonably foreseeable is the most vulnerable.

### What distinguishes legitimate financing

- A real lender advancing real money, ideally an independent institution.
- Commercial terms: a market interest rate, a stated maturity and the remedies a third-party lender would require.
- Actual performance: payments made on schedule and defaults handled as an independent lender would.
- Proper recording and perfection before any claim arises.
- A legitimate business, investment or estate planning purpose, and a borrower who remains solvent.

### Pros and cons

- **Pros.** Can reduce the equity exposed in assets that are difficult to retitle, such as a home or operating property; relies on established secured-lending law; and can fit within a broader financing and estate plan.
- **Cons.** Real debt carries real cost and risk, including interest expense, refinancing and maturity risk, and foreclosure if values fall or payments stop; existing loan covenants may restrict further borrowing; related-party arrangements draw scrutiny; and the borrowed funds themselves remain exposed unless separately protected under applicable law.

### Illustrative example

An owner holds an unencumbered rental building worth \$3 million. Years before any claim, and while solvent, the owner obtains a \$2.2 million loan from an unrelated commercial lender secured by the building and uses the proceeds to acquire another investment property in a separate entity. If a later judgment arises, the lender’s senior lien is paid first from any sale of the first building, leaving roughly \$800,000 of equity, less sale costs, for the judgment creditor. The second property and the owner’s interest in its entity are separate assets whose exposure depends on entity and state law.

Illustrative only. The result depends on state law, lien priority, timing, solvency and the facts.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is equity stripping legal?**

Borrowing against one’s own property is ordinary and lawful. Whether a particular financing can be avoided or recharacterized depends on several factors, including the borrower’s intent, the value exchanged, the borrower’s financial condition, the timing relative to any claim, the relationship between the parties and the way the debt is documented and serviced. No single factor is decisive.

**Q: Can I borrow from my own company or trust?**

Sometimes, but related-party loans receive the closest scrutiny and may be recharacterized or avoided even when documented on commercial terms. Independent financing is far more reliable.

**Q: Does a recorded lien always take priority?**

No. Earlier liens, certain statutory and tax liens, and the possibility that the lien itself is avoided all affect priority.

**Q: What happens if property values fall?**

The borrower remains liable for the debt. Any financing should leave a margin of safety and be sized to cash flow the borrower can sustain.

## 7. Charitable giving strategies compared: from donor-advised funds to NIMCRUTs

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/charitable-giving-strategies/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/charitable-giving-strategies
- **Category:** Tax strategy (Tax strategy · Philanthropy)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** Outright gifts, appreciated stock, donor-advised funds, private foundations, charitable remainder trusts including the NIMCRUT and flip unitrust, lead trusts, gift annuities and IRA gifts, with the 2026 rule changes.

### The short answer

A common charitable strategy is a gift of publicly traded stock held more than a year, which generally avoids capital gains tax on the appreciation and is deductible at fair market value, within percentage-of-income limits; gifts must be completed before any sale is arranged. Donor-advised funds add timing flexibility; private foundations add control at higher cost. Charitable remainder trusts pay the donor an income stream, defer tax on the sale of appreciated assets and leave the remainder to charity; the NIMCRUT variant pays only the trust’s accounting income up to a stated percentage, with shortfalls tracked and potentially paid later, which some donors use to shift income into retirement years. Charitable lead trusts do the reverse, paying charity first and passing the remainder to family. From 2026, new floors and caps reduce the deduction for many itemizers, which makes structure matter more.

### What changed in 2026

The 2025 tax law made several changes that take effect in 2026. Itemized charitable deductions now count only to the extent they exceed 0.5 percent of adjusted gross income. For taxpayers in the top bracket, the value of itemized deductions is capped at a 35 percent benefit. Taxpayers who take the standard deduction can deduct up to \$1,000 of cash gifts (\$2,000 for joint filers). The 60 percent of AGI limit on cash gifts to public charities is now permanent, and the federal estate and gift tax exemption is \$15 million per person. The 35 percent cap operates through a revised overall limitation on itemized deductions, so its effect depends on the taxpayer’s whole return.

The effect is to reward larger, planned gifts over small annual ones, and to make structures that avoid capital gains tax, or that work outside the income tax deduction entirely, relatively more valuable.

### Outright gifts of cash and appreciated assets

A cash gift to a public charity is deductible up to 60 percent of AGI. A gift of long-term capital gain property to a public charity is generally deductible at fair market value, up to 30 percent of AGI, and the built-in gain is not taxed to the donor. Excess deductions carry forward five years. The rules narrow in several common situations:

- Short-term and ordinary-income property is generally deductible only at its cost basis.
- Tangible personal property, such as art, is deductible at fair market value only if the charity’s use is related to its exempt purpose.
- Gifts to private foundations of property other than qualified appreciated publicly traded stock are generally deductible only at basis.
- Gifts of encumbered property can be treated partly as a sale.

**Assignment of income.** If a sale of the asset is already arranged or legally binding when the gift is made, as is common with an interest in a business under a signed purchase agreement, the IRS can tax the donor on the gain as if the donor had sold. Gifts of a business interest, or transfers to a charitable remainder trust, should be completed well before a sale is committed.

**Pros.** Simple; for qualifying appreciated assets, a double benefit. **Cons.** No retained income or control; gifts of real estate, private stock and other illiquid assets require qualified appraisals and a charity willing to accept them.

### Donor-advised funds

A donor-advised fund is an account at a sponsoring public charity. The donor takes the deduction when assets are contributed and recommends grants to charities over time.

**Pros.** Immediate deduction with deferred grant-making, useful for “bunching” several years of giving into one high-income year; accepts appreciated and some illiquid assets; low cost; public-charity deduction limits. **Cons.** The sponsor has legal control of the assets; grants cannot provide benefits to the donor; and IRA qualified charitable distributions cannot be made to a donor-advised fund.

### Private foundations

A private foundation is a separate charitable entity the family controls, typically with family members as directors.

**Pros.** Permanent family control over investments and grants; a vehicle for family governance and legacy; can employ family members at reasonable compensation. **Cons.** Lower deduction limits (generally 30 percent of AGI for cash and 20 percent for appreciated property, with most non-publicly traded property deductible only at cost basis); a 1.39 percent excise tax on net investment income; a required minimum distribution of about 5 percent of assets each year; strict self-dealing rules; and annual public filings.

### Charitable remainder trusts

A charitable remainder trust (CRT) pays the donor, or other individuals, an annual amount for life or for a term of up to 20 years, and gives what remains to charity. The trust is generally exempt from income tax, so it can sell appreciated property without immediate capital gains tax and reinvest the full proceeds; if it has unrelated business taxable income, however, a 100 percent excise tax applies to that income. The donor receives a deduction for the present value of the charity’s remainder, which must be at least 10 percent of the amount contributed. The annual payout must be between 5 and 50 percent.

- **Charitable remainder annuity trust (CRAT).** Pays a fixed dollar amount each year. Predictable, but no additional contributions are allowed, and a CRAT measured by lives must pass a test showing no more than a 5 percent probability that the trust will be exhausted, or include the early-termination provision permitted by Revenue Procedure 2016-42.
- **Standard charitable remainder unitrust (CRUT).** Pays a fixed percentage of the trust’s value, revalued each year, so payments rise and fall with the portfolio. Additional contributions are permitted if the trust instrument provides for them.
- **Net income unitrust (NICRUT).** Pays the lesser of the stated percentage or the trust’s actual income. Shortfalls are not made up.
- **Net income with makeup unitrust (NIMCRUT).** Pays the lesser of the stated percentage or the trust’s net accounting income, and keeps a cumulative record of past shortfalls that can be paid in later years when accounting income exceeds the stated percentage.
- **Flip unitrust.** Begins as a net income trust and converts to a standard unitrust after a triggering event permitted by the regulations, such as a specific date, a beneficiary’s marriage, divorce, death or birth of a child, or the sale of an unmarketable asset such as real estate. The event must be outside the control of the trustee and any other person, and the conversion takes effect at the start of the following year.

Payments to the beneficiary are taxed under a tiered system: ordinary income first, then capital gains, then tax-exempt income, then a return of principal. A CRT therefore defers tax rather than eliminating it.

### The NIMCRUT in more detail

A NIMCRUT pays only what the trust earns as *accounting income* under its governing instrument and state law, which is not the same as taxable income or cash flow. Capital gains, for example, are generally allocated to principal rather than income unless the instrument and state law provide otherwise. Because investment choices affect how much accounting income the trust produces, a NIMCRUT invested for growth may pay little in its early years, and the cumulative shortfall (the makeup amount) grows. If accounting income later exceeds the stated percentage, the excess can be paid out toward that shortfall.

The makeup amount is a running calculation, not a segregated or guaranteed fund: it can be paid only from future excess accounting income, and if the trust never produces that income, or loses value, it may never be paid. Investment decisions belong to the trustee, who owes fiduciary duties to both the income beneficiary and the charity; arrangements that let the donor control the timing of income at will invite IRS scrutiny and self-dealing concerns, which is why an independent trustee is commonly used.

**Common uses.** Supplementing retirement income for business owners and professionals who have already maximized qualified plans; receiving income over time from appreciated property contributed well before any sale is arranged; and, in a flip design, holding real estate or other unmarketable assets until they sell and then converting to a regular payout.

**Pros.** Tax-deferred growth and reinvestment of the full sale proceeds; flexibility over when income is received; a current deduction; and a charitable legacy.

**Cons.** Irrevocable, with the remainder committed to charity; payments depend on the trust’s definition of accounting income and on investment decisions made by a fiduciary; the makeup amount is never guaranteed; and the IRS has historically scrutinized income-timing arrangements, so the structure must be conservative and properly administered.

### Charitable lead trusts

A charitable lead trust reverses the CRT: charity receives payments for a term, and the remainder passes to family. In a non-grantor lead trust, the gift to family is valued at a steep discount, and in a “zeroed-out” design it can be close to zero, so growth above the IRS’s Section 7520 rate passes to heirs free of gift tax. A grantor lead trust instead gives the donor an upfront income tax deduction, with the trust’s income taxed to the donor afterward.

**Pros.** Transfers appreciation to the next generation at low transfer-tax cost while supporting charity. **Cons.** Works best when interest rates are low and returns high; heirs wait for the lead term to end; and the arithmetic must be modeled carefully.

### Charitable gift annuities and pooled income funds

A charitable gift annuity is a contract under which a charity pays a fixed lifetime annuity in exchange for a gift, with part of the gift deductible. It is simple and inexpensive but is backed only by the charity’s general credit. Pooled income funds, maintained by some larger charities, combine many donors’ gifts and pay each a share of the fund’s income.

### Qualified charitable distributions from IRAs

Individuals aged 70½ or older can transfer up to \$111,000 in 2026 directly from an IRA to eligible public charities. The distribution is excluded from income rather than deducted, so it is not affected by the new 0.5 percent floor or 35 percent cap, and it can satisfy required minimum distributions. It cannot be made to a donor-advised fund, supporting organization or most private foundations. A separate, one-time election allows a limited, inflation-adjusted amount to fund a charitable gift annuity or charitable remainder trust.

### Choosing among them

| Goal                                         | Strategies to consider                                   |
|----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Simple tax-efficient gift                    | Long-held publicly traded stock; IRA gifts after 70½     |
| Deduction now, grants later                  | Donor-advised fund                                       |
| Family control and legacy                    | Private foundation                                       |
| Sell an appreciated asset and receive income | Charitable remainder trust (CRUT, CRAT or flip unitrust) |
| Income weighted toward later years           | NIMCRUT, with an independent trustee                     |
| Pass appreciation to heirs                   | Charitable lead trust                                    |

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Does a charitable remainder trust eliminate capital gains tax?**

No. It defers it. The trust sells without immediate tax, but distributions carry out the gain to the beneficiary over time under the tiered taxation rules.

**Q: Can I be the trustee of my own charitable remainder trust?**

Often yes, but for a NIMCRUT or a trust holding hard-to-value assets, an independent trustee or special trustee is usually advisable to avoid self-dealing and valuation problems.

**Q: What is the 10 percent remainder test?**

The present value of the charity’s remainder interest, calculated using IRS tables, must be at least 10 percent of the value contributed. Young beneficiaries, long terms and high payouts can cause a trust to fail it.

**Q: Is a donor-advised fund or a private foundation better?**

A donor-advised fund is simpler, cheaper and offers higher deduction limits. A private foundation offers control and permanence at greater cost and regulation. Some families use both.

## 8. Oil and gas investments as a tax strategy: how they work, and their limits

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/oil-and-gas-tax-strategy/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/oil-and-gas-tax-strategy
- **Category:** Tax strategy (Tax strategy · Energy)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How direct oil and gas investments generate deductions through intangible drilling costs, depreciation and depletion, when those deductions can offset other income, and the limits, recapture and investment risks that come with them.

### The short answer

A direct investment in oil and gas drilling, usually as a working interest, can produce large first-year deductions: qualifying operators and working interest owners may generally elect to expense eligible intangible drilling costs (IDCs), equipment is depreciated (with 100 percent bonus depreciation available for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025), and production income may be partly sheltered by depletion. A working interest held without limited liability is treated as nonpassive, so its losses can offset other income, but only after the basis, at-risk and excess business loss limits are applied, and the benefit must also be tested under the alternative minimum tax. Deductions can be recaptured on sale, and the investment itself carries drilling, price, liquidity and operator risk that matter more than the tax benefit.

### How does an oil and gas investment create deductions?

Drilling a well involves three kinds of cost, each treated differently:

- **Intangible drilling and development costs (IDCs).** Labor, fuel, chemicals, mud, site preparation and other costs with no salvage value. Under Section 263(c) and Treasury Regulation 1.612-4, qualifying operators and working interest owners may generally elect to expense eligible IDCs rather than capitalize them, subject to the applicable rules and limitations; whether a particular cost qualifies depends on its nature and the taxpayer’s election and accounting method. For many wells, IDCs are the majority of drilling cost. Integrated oil companies must capitalize 30 percent of their IDCs under Section 291(b) and recover that amount over 60 months, so it is deferred rather than lost. A taxpayer who has elected to expense IDCs deducts them whether or not the well succeeds; a taxpayer who capitalizes IDCs may, under the regulations, deduct those relating to a dry hole when it is abandoned. Not every cost associated with an unsuccessful project is automatically deductible.
- **Tangible equipment.** Casing, wellhead equipment, pumps and tanks are depreciated, generally as seven-year property, although individual assets can be classified differently. Property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 may qualify for 100 percent bonus depreciation under Section 168(k), subject to its acquisition, prior-use, related-party and other requirements.
- **Lease and acquisition costs.** The cost of acquiring the lease or mineral rights is a separate category from drilling costs: it produces no IDC deduction and is recovered through depletion as the property produces.

Once a well produces, the owner reports its share of revenue less operating costs. **Depletion** then shelters part of that income. *Cost depletion* recovers the owner’s adjusted depletable basis in proportion to production relative to estimated recoverable reserves. *Percentage depletion*, available under Section 613A only to qualifying independent producers and royalty owners (subject to its related-party, transfer and retailer and refiner exclusions), allows a deduction of generally 15 percent of gross income from the property, but:

- it is limited to 100 percent of the taxable income from each property, computed under its own rules;
- the 1,000-barrel average daily production limit applies to the taxpayer as a whole (with natural gas converted at the statutory equivalent of 6,000 cubic feet per barrel);
- total percentage depletion cannot exceed 65 percent of the taxpayer’s taxable income, computed with statutory adjustments, with any amount disallowed by that limit generally carried forward.

The owner claims the greater of cost or percentage depletion, and percentage depletion can continue after basis has been fully recovered.

### Can the deductions offset ordinary income?

Sometimes. Losses from activities in which an investor does not materially participate are generally passive and can offset only passive income. Section 469(c)(3) makes an exception for a **working interest** in an oil or gas property held directly or through an entity that does not limit the owner’s liability: such an interest is treated as nonpassive without the ordinary material participation tests, so its losses can offset wages, business income and investment income. Other oil and gas activities can also be nonpassive if the owner materially participates.

Some drilling programs use general partnership structures for this reason. Their treatment depends on whether the investor’s liability is genuinely unlimited during the relevant period under the regulations (Treasury Regulation 1.469-1T(e)(4)). A later conversion to limited partner status requires separate analysis and does not necessarily eliminate liabilities incurred earlier. Under Section 469(c)(3)(B), if losses from a property were treated as nonpassive under the working interest exception, later net income from that same property is also treated as nonpassive.

Several separate limits apply, generally in this order:

1.  **Basis.** A partner can deduct losses only up to the adjusted basis of its partnership interest under Section 704(d) (or, for a direct owner, its basis in the property).
2.  **At-risk.** Losses are further limited to the amount at risk under Section 465, which generally excludes nonrecourse financing, subject to exceptions.
3.  **Passive activity.** The Section 469 rules described above.
4.  **Excess business loss.** For noncorporate taxpayers, Section 461(l), now permanent, generally limits aggregate net losses from all trades or businesses, after netting business income and gains, to an annual threshold of \$256,000 (\$512,000 for joint filers) for 2026 under Revenue Procedure 2025-32. Wages are not treated as business income for this purpose. Disallowed excess business losses generally become net operating loss carryforwards.

**Alternative minimum tax** is a separate computation rather than a further step. Under Section 57(a)(2), excess IDCs can be a tax preference for AMT purposes; independent producers receive special treatment that limits the preference, subject to statutory conditions. The benefit of a drilling investment should be modeled under both regular tax and AMT.

A **royalty interest**, by contrast, bears no drilling or operating costs and produces no IDC deduction, although royalty owners may bear production taxes and, depending on the lease, certain post-production charges. Royalty income is generally portfolio income for passive activity purposes, unless derived in the ordinary course of a trade or business, and may be partly sheltered by depletion if the owner qualifies.

### Timing and prepayments

The timing of deductions for prepaid drilling costs depends on the taxpayer’s accounting method, the economic performance rules and applicable special rules. Section 461(i)(2) provides a limited exception for certain drilling expenditures where drilling begins within 90 days after the close of the taxable year. Merely paying a drilling program before year-end does not establish that the payment is deductible that year. Investors should have their own adviser confirm the program’s drilling schedule and the basis for any year-end deduction rather than rely on offering materials.

### Recapture on sale

Deductions taken now can be recaptured later. On a sale of the property, previously deducted IDCs and depletion that reduced basis are generally recaptured as ordinary income under Section 1254, and depreciation on equipment under Section 1245, in each case to the extent of the gain. A sale of a partnership interest rather than the underlying property can produce similar ordinary income through Section 751. The benefit is therefore partly a deferral, which is most valuable when deductions are taken at a high rate and income is recognized later at a lower one.

### Illustrative example

A married couple filing jointly, with \$1.2 million of wage and investment income and no other business income or losses, invests \$500,000 as general partners in a drilling program in 2026. Suppose the program allocates them \$375,000 of deductible IDCs and \$60,000 of bonus depreciation in the first year, all within their basis and amount at risk, with no production income allocated that year. Their \$435,000 loss is below the \$512,000 excess business loss threshold. If the entire deduction offsets income otherwise taxable at a 37 percent marginal federal rate, the illustrative tax reduction is \$160,950; actual savings depend on the character of the income, the couple’s brackets, AMT and other limits.

Even under these assumptions, the tax benefit reduces only part of the investors’ economic exposure: they have committed \$500,000 and remain at risk of losing it. If the wells produce, later income is partly sheltered by depletion; on a sale, IDCs, depletion and depreciation are recaptured as ordinary income to the extent of the gain.

Illustrative only. Allocations, timing, state tax, the investors’ other income and losses, the program’s actual results and many other rules affect the outcome. A tax benefit does not make an unsound investment sound.

### Investment risks

- **Drilling risk.** Wells can be dry or produce less than projected.
- **Commodity risk.** Oil and gas prices are volatile and drive both income and value.
- **Liquidity.** Program interests and working interests are generally illiquid for many years.
- **Operator and sponsor risk.** Results depend heavily on the operator’s skill, costs and integrity, and on program fees.
- **Liability.** A working interest owner, or a general partner in a program, can be exposed to operational and environmental liabilities that exceed the amount invested. Insurance can reduce some exposures but does not cover every liability, and converting to limited partner status does not necessarily extinguish obligations incurred earlier.
- **Securities law.** Interests in drilling programs may be securities subject to federal and state securities laws. Many private offerings rely on exemptions from registration and limit participation to eligible investors. The offering’s structure, and the registration or licensing status of any intermediary involved, should be verified independently.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: How much of an investment is deductible in the first year?**

It depends on the program’s mix of intangible and tangible costs, when drilling occurs, and the investor’s basis, at-risk amount and other losses. Program projections should be confirmed by the investor’s own tax adviser.

**Q: Do oil and gas losses always offset wages and business income?**

No. A qualifying working interest held directly or through an entity that does not limit the owner’s liability is nonpassive without meeting the ordinary material participation tests, and other oil and gas activities can be nonpassive through material participation. In either case losses remain subject to the basis, at-risk and excess business loss limits, and to AMT.

**Q: Can an oil and gas investment eliminate my income tax?**

Not necessarily. Qualifying investments can generate substantial deductions, but their use depends on the ownership structure, income, basis, amount at risk, passive activity treatment, excess business loss limits and AMT. The tax benefit should be evaluated separately from the investment’s economic merits.

**Q: Is a royalty interest a tax shelter?**

Not in the same way. It produces no drilling deductions; its tax advantage is percentage depletion on the income it produces.

**Q: What happens when I sell?**

Previously deducted IDCs and depletion that reduced basis are generally recaptured as ordinary income under Section 1254, and equipment depreciation under Section 1245, to the extent of the gain. Selling a partnership interest can produce similar results through Section 751.

## 9. Cost segregation and bonus depreciation for real estate and agricultural property

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/cost-segregation-and-bonus-depreciation/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/cost-segregation-and-bonus-depreciation
- **Category:** Real estate (Real estate · Tax strategy · Agriculture)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How a cost segregation study reclassifies parts of a building into shorter-lived property eligible for 100 percent bonus depreciation, who can use the resulting deductions, how farm and ranch property differs, and the recapture, audit and planning considerations.

### The short answer

A cost segregation study is an engineering-based analysis that separates qualifying components of a building, such as dedicated systems serving specific equipment, certain finishes and fixtures, and site improvements, from the structure itself. Qualifying components are depreciated over 5, 7 or 15 years instead of 27.5 years (residential rental) or 39 years (nonresidential), and property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 may qualify for 100 percent bonus depreciation, so a significant share of a building’s cost can be deducted in its first year. Whether the deduction offsets other income depends on basis and at-risk limits, the passive activity rules (including real estate professional and short-term rental rules), personal use and the excess business loss limit. Accelerated depreciation is recaptured on sale, and it is not always the best structure for a particular owner’s goals.

### What is cost segregation?

Without a study, the entire depreciable cost of a building (its purchase price or construction cost, less land) is generally recovered on a straight-line basis over 27.5 years for residential rental property or 39 years for nonresidential property. Many parts of a building, however, are not “structural” for tax purposes. A cost segregation study, performed by engineers and tax specialists, identifies those components and their costs:

- **5- and 7-year personal property:** items such as removable carpeting, certain cabinetry and millwork, decorative fixtures, appliances, and electrical, plumbing or other systems dedicated to qualifying equipment or specific business functions. General building systems, such as ordinary electrical and plumbing serving the building as a whole, ordinarily remain part of the building.
- **15-year land improvements:** parking lots, sidewalks, landscaping, fencing, site drainage and outdoor lighting.
- **Qualified improvement property:** under Section 168(e)(6), improvements to the interior of nonresidential real property made after the building was first placed in service, excluding enlargements, elevators and escalators, and the internal structural framework; also 15-year property.

How much is reclassified varies widely with the property type, its construction, the purchase price allocation and the components present. The IRS’s Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide describes the methodology and documentation it expects from a quality study and gives examples of how particular components are classified.

### How bonus depreciation multiplies the effect

Many assets with a MACRS recovery period of 20 years or less qualify for bonus depreciation under Section 168(k), subject to its acquisition, original-use or used-property, placed-in-service and other requirements and exclusions. The 2025 tax law made 100 percent bonus depreciation permanent for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Property acquired earlier, including under a written binding contract entered into on or before that date, remains subject to the earlier phase-down, and special rules apply to self-constructed property and components; the closing date alone does not determine eligibility. Qualifying components identified in a study can therefore often be deducted entirely in the year the property is placed in service, rather than over 5 to 15 years.

Section 179 expensing is a separate, elective route for certain property and is not interchangeable with bonus depreciation. For tax years beginning in 2026 the maximum deduction is \$2,560,000, reduced dollar for dollar once qualifying property placed in service exceeds \$4,090,000 (Revenue Procedure 2025-32). The deduction cannot exceed taxable income from the active conduct of a trade or business, and noncorporate lessors face additional restrictions.

### The business interest election and ADS

A real property trade or business can elect out of the business interest limitation of Section 163(j). The price of that election is that its nonresidential real property, residential rental property and qualified improvement property must be depreciated under the alternative depreciation system (ADS), over longer recovery periods, and property required to use ADS is not eligible for bonus depreciation. Personal property and land improvements identified in a study are generally unaffected, but the election changes the overall comparison and should be modeled before a study is relied on.

### Studies on property already owned

A study need not be done at purchase. For property already owned, a qualifying study may support a change in depreciation accounting method through Form 3115. Where that procedure is available, a Section 481(a) adjustment lets previously missed depreciation be recognized in the year of change without amending prior returns; other circumstances may require a different correction. The bonus depreciation percentage generally depends on when the property was acquired and placed in service, not the year the study is performed.

### Who can use the deductions?

The deduction is only as valuable as the owner’s ability to use it. Several separate limits apply, generally in this order:

- **Basis and at-risk.** An owner of a partnership, LLC or S corporation can deduct losses only to the extent of its basis in the entity, and losses are further limited by the at-risk rules of Section 465. Qualified nonrecourse financing secured by real property is generally treated as at risk.
- **Passive investors.** Rental real estate is generally a passive activity, so losses offset only passive income. Unused passive losses carry forward and can offset future passive income, and they generally become deductible in full upon a fully taxable disposition of the taxpayer’s entire interest in the activity to an unrelated person under Section 469(g). Installment sales, related-party transfers and tax-deferred exchanges can produce different results.
- **Real estate professionals.** Under Section 469(c)(7) and Treasury Regulation 1.469-9, a taxpayer qualifies only if both tests are met: more than 750 hours a year, and more than half of all personal services performed in trades or businesses, in real property businesses in which the taxpayer materially participates. Services as an employee generally count only if the taxpayer owns more than 5 percent of the employer. On a joint return, one spouse must meet both tests alone, although the other spouse’s work can count toward material participation. The taxpayer must also materially participate in the rental itself; each rental is tested separately unless an election is made to treat all rentals as one activity.
- **Short-term rentals.** A property with an average guest stay of seven days or less, or of 30 days or less with significant personal services, is excluded from the definition of a rental activity under the passive activity regulations. That does not make its losses nonpassive automatically: the owner must still materially participate in the activity.
- **Personal use.** Under Section 280A, using a dwelling unit personally for more than the greater of 14 days or 10 percent of the days it is rented can limit deductions to rental income, even where the other tests are met.
- **Excess business loss limit.** For noncorporate taxpayers, Section 461(l) generally limits aggregate net losses from all trades or businesses, after netting business income and gains, to \$256,000 (\$512,000 for joint filers) for 2026 under Revenue Procedure 2025-32. Wages are not treated as business income for this purpose. Disallowed excess business losses generally become net operating loss carryforwards.
- **State conformity.** Some states, including California, do not follow federal bonus depreciation, so state deductions follow a slower schedule.

### Agricultural property

Farms and ranches have their own recovery periods, and much of their value often sits in assets that cost segregation and bonus depreciation can accelerate:

- **Land** is never depreciable, so allocating purchase price between land and improvements is the first and most important step.
- **Single-purpose agricultural and horticultural structures** are generally 10-year property, but only if the structure meets the statutory design and use requirements (Sections 168(i)(13) and 1245(a)(3)(D)); not every livestock building or greenhouse qualifies. General-purpose farm buildings are generally 20-year property.
- **Land improvements.** Depreciable agricultural land improvements, including qualifying drainage, irrigation, fencing and access infrastructure, may receive shorter recovery periods than farm buildings. The appropriate classification depends on the specific asset, its function and the applicable guidance (Revenue Procedure 87-56, IRS Publications 225 and 946), and some permanent land improvements without a determinable useful life are not depreciable at all.
- **Machinery and equipment** used in farming is generally 7-year property. New machinery and equipment whose original use begins with the taxpayer after 2017 is 5-year property, but that rule excludes grain bins, cotton ginning assets, fences and other land improvements, and used machinery keeps its ordinary classification.
- **Trees and vines.** Orchards and vineyards involve several distinct situations. Buying an existing productive orchard means depreciating the purchased trees or vines under the ordinary rules. Planting new fruit- or nut-bearing trees or vines allows a special election under Section 168(k)(5) to take bonus depreciation in the year they are planted or grafted, rather than when they become productive. Expenditures during the preproductive period are generally capitalized under Section 263A, subject to exceptions and elections for certain farmers. Bare agricultural land is not depreciable.
- **Qualified production property.** The 2025 law added Section 168(n), an elective deduction for qualifying nonresidential real property used by the taxpayer as an integral part of specified domestic production activities, potentially including qualifying agricultural production, generally where construction begins after January 19, 2025 and before 2029 and the property is placed in service before 2031. Eligibility depends on the property’s actual use, original use or acquisition history, placed-in-service date and other requirements. Office, administrative, lodging, parking and other non-production areas are excluded, property leased to others generally does not qualify, the deduction must be elected, and it can be recaptured after certain changes in use. Ordinary ownership or leasing of agricultural property does not automatically qualify; Treasury and IRS guidance should be checked.

Because farm purchases often bundle land, buildings, improvements, equipment and sometimes plantings, a careful allocation and a study can move a meaningful share of the price into short recovery periods. Passive activity, material participation and excess business loss rules apply to farm owners as well. A landowner who leases to an unrelated operator for cash rent is often passive, but crop-share and other lease structures, material participation, and self-rental rules where the owner also operates the farm can change the result.

> A note from Paratum
>
> Cost segregation paired with bonus depreciation is often a sound move, but it is not automatically the right one. Depending on an owner’s goals and fact pattern, such as a planned sale, a 1031 exchange, an estate plan that relies on a basis adjustment at death, the owner’s passive or real estate professional status, or state tax exposure, alternative structures may fit better. Accelerating deductions into a year when they cannot be used, or ahead of a sale that recaptures them, can produce less benefit than expected. We look at accelerated depreciation alongside those alternatives before recommending it.

### Recapture and other costs

- **Recapture on sale.** Accelerated depreciation may increase the ordinary-income recapture or other depreciation-related gain recognized on a later sale. Gain on Section 1245 property is generally ordinary income up to the depreciation taken, including bonus depreciation. Certain real property improvements are instead Section 1250 property, where ordinary recapture is generally limited to depreciation in excess of straight-line, and remaining gain attributable to depreciation (unrecaptured Section 1250 gain) is taxed to individuals at up to 25 percent. The result depends on each asset’s classification, adjusted basis, depreciation history and the sale price allocated to it.
- **Like-kind exchanges.** Since 2018, Section 1031 applies only to real property. A qualifying exchange may defer certain gain, but cost segregation complicates the analysis: components treated as personal property for depreciation may or may not be real property for exchange purposes under Treasury Regulation 1.1031(a)-3, and Sections 1245(b)(4) and 1250(d)(4) govern recapture in exchanges. The classification of relinquished and replacement assets, the allocation of consideration and the recapture rules must each be evaluated.
- **Basis adjustment at death.** Property that receives a basis adjustment under Section 1014 may avoid tax on previously deducted depreciation. The result depends on ownership and estate inclusion; for property held in a partnership, a basis adjustment to the inherited partnership interest does not by itself adjust the partnership’s basis in its assets unless a Section 754 election produces a Section 743(b) adjustment.
- **California and other states.** Because California does not follow federal bonus depreciation, federal and California adjusted bases differ, producing different depreciation deductions each year and different gain on a sale.
- **Study cost and audit risk.** Studies cost money and must withstand IRS review. Aggressive allocations without engineering support are a common audit issue.
- **Partial dispositions.** When a component is replaced, the remaining basis of the old component may be deductible, but partial disposition treatment can require an election and a reasonable allocation of basis under Treasury Regulation 1.168(i)-8. Identification in a study does not by itself establish a deductible loss.

### Illustrative example

An investor acquires a multifamily rental property and places it in service in January 2026 for \$5 million, of which \$1 million is allocated to land. A study reclassifies \$1 million of the \$4 million building basis into 5-, 7- and 15-year property eligible for 100 percent bonus depreciation. Using the mid-month convention for the building:

- **Without a study:** first-year depreciation of about \$139,394 (\$4 million over 27.5 years, for 11.5 months).
- **With a study:** about \$1,104,545 (\$1 million of bonus depreciation plus \$104,545 on the remaining \$3 million).
- **Incremental first-year depreciation:** about \$965,151.

That is a difference in deductions, not in tax savings. Whether the deduction reduces taxes now depends on the investor’s basis, at-risk amount, passive or real estate professional status and the excess business loss limit. On a later sale, the depreciation taken on the reclassified components may be recaptured as ordinary income, depending on each component’s classification and the gain allocated to it.

Illustrative only, federal tax only, using exact mid-month fractions rather than rounded IRS table percentages. Results depend on the property, the study, the owner’s tax position and holding period.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is cost segregation worth it for a small property?**

It depends on the depreciable basis, the share likely to be reclassified, the owner’s ability to use the deductions and the expected holding period. Studies are commonly considered once a building’s depreciable basis reaches several hundred thousand dollars or more.

**Q: Can I do a study on a building I bought years ago?**

Often, yes. Where the accounting method change procedure is available, missed depreciation can be recognized in the year of change through Form 3115 and a Section 481(a) adjustment, without amending prior returns. The bonus depreciation rate depends on when the property was acquired and placed in service.

**Q: Do the deductions offset my salary?**

Only if the losses survive the basis and at-risk limits and are nonpassive, for example through real estate professional status with material participation in the rental, or a short-term rental in which the owner materially participates, and then only within the excess business loss limit and any personal-use limits.

**Q: Is cost segregation permanent tax savings or tax deferral?**

Generally deferral. It accelerates depreciation rather than increasing the total depreciable basis, so its economic benefit usually comes from receiving deductions earlier. Differences in tax rates over time, the way the property is eventually disposed of, and basis adjustments at death can change the ultimate outcome.

**Q: Can buying farmland reduce my ordinary income taxes?**

Land itself is not depreciable. Qualifying farm buildings, equipment, irrigation infrastructure and other improvements can generate depreciation, and certain production property and newly planted trees or vines may receive special treatment. Whether those deductions reduce other income depends on the owner’s participation, ownership structure and the applicable loss limits.

**Q: Does cost segregation apply to farms?**

Yes. Farm buildings, land improvements, irrigation, fences and equipment have their own recovery periods, and a careful allocation of the purchase price can accelerate a large part of the cost. Land itself is never depreciable.
