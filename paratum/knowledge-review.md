## 1. Can net operating losses be monetized?

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/can-net-operating-losses-be-monetized/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/can-net-operating-losses-be-monetized
- **Category:** M&A and transactions (Corporate taxation · Transactions)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** Net operating losses cannot be sold on their own, but transactions can let a profitable business use them, within the limits of Sections 172, 269, 382, 383 and 384 and the economic substance doctrine.

### The short answer

Not on their own. A net operating loss (NOL) is a tax attribute of the taxpayer that incurred it and cannot be sold or assigned separately. Its value can be realized only through transactions that let real taxable income be offset by the loss, most often by bringing a profitable business or income-producing assets into the loss company, or by acquiring the loss company. Every such transaction is subject to the limits of Sections 172, 269, 382, 383 and 384 of the Internal Revenue Code and to the economic substance doctrine, and must make sense apart from its tax benefits.

### What is a net operating loss?

A net operating loss arises when a business’s allowable deductions exceed its income for the year. Its treatment depends on when it arose:

- **Losses arising in tax years beginning after 2017** generally carry forward indefinitely and, for tax years beginning after 2020, can offset no more than 80 percent of taxable income (computed before the NOL deduction).
- **Losses arising in 2018, 2019 and 2020** could, under temporary CARES Act rules, be carried back five years, subject to elections to waive the carryback.
- **Losses arising before 2018** generally retain the former rules: a 20-year carryforward and no 80 percent limit.
- **Special rules** apply to farming losses and certain insurance company losses.

A company holding losses from both before and after 2018 must apply them in order and apply the 80 percent limit only to the later losses, so the usable amount in any year depends on the mix.

An NOL is a tax attribute: a right to reduce future taxable income, but only if the company that holds it earns that income. In financial statements it may support a deferred tax asset under ASC 740, but only to the extent its realization is judged more likely than not.

### Can NOLs be sold on their own?

No. Tax attributes belong to the taxpayer that generated them. A corporation cannot sell or assign its NOLs to another company. An individual’s NOL generally cannot be transferred to another person either: losses on a joint return must be allocated between spouses, and a decedent’s unused NOL can generally be used only on the decedent’s final return, including a final joint return with a surviving spouse, after which it expires.

What can change hands is the company that holds the losses, or the income that the losses can shelter. Every NOL transaction is built on one of those two ideas.

> In practice · Aaron Sokol, Principal
>
> Some of the most frequent calls we receive are from individuals with large losses they hope to sell, often after a business that didn’t work out or a hard year in the markets. Most are surprised to learn that a loss, on its own, can’t be sold or handed to someone else. A loss has value only to the taxpayer that owns it, and only when it is used inside a real business with real income. Any transaction built around it has to make economic sense on its own terms, apart from the tax benefit. When those conditions can be met, the path generally follows the structures described in this article. When they can’t, the most valuable advice is often to keep the loss where it is and plan around it.

### How does Section 382 limit NOLs after an ownership change?

Section 382 applies when a loss corporation undergoes an *ownership change*. Under Section 382(g) and the regulations, an ownership change occurs when the percentage of stock owned by one or more 5-percent shareholders increases by more than 50 percentage points over the lowest percentage those shareholders owned at any time during the testing period, which is generally three years. Detailed attribution, aggregation, option and public-group rules determine who counts as a 5-percent shareholder and how ownership is measured.

After an ownership change, losses that existed before the change can offset only a limited amount of income each year. The annual limit is generally the value of the loss corporation’s stock immediately before the change multiplied by the long-term tax-exempt rate, which the IRS publishes monthly. That value is adjusted for capital contributions made to inflate it, redemptions and other corporate contractions, and substantial holdings of nonbusiness assets. In the year of the change the limit is prorated, and any unused portion carries forward to later years.

Two further rules often decide the outcome:

- **Continuity of business enterprise.** If the new loss corporation does not continue the old loss corporation’s business enterprise for two years after the change, the annual limit generally becomes zero, so the pre-change losses become effectively unusable, apart from limited increases for certain recognized built-in gains.
- **Built-in gains and losses.** If the company had a net unrealized built-in gain at the time of the change, built-in gains recognized during the five-year recognition period can increase the annual limit. If it had a net unrealized built-in loss, built-in losses recognized during that period are treated as pre-change losses and become subject to the limit themselves. Threshold rules determine whether either regime applies.

Section 383 applies parallel limits to tax credits and capital loss carryforwards.

### What transaction structures may be available?

- **Bringing income to the losses.** A loss company acquires a profitable business or income-producing assets for cash, or for stock that does not cause an ownership change. Section 382 is not triggered by the acquisition itself, but the losses remain subject to any existing Section 382 limit from earlier changes, the 80 percent limit, Section 269, Section 384, consolidated return rules and the economic substance doctrine. Avoiding a new ownership change does not make the losses freely usable.
- **Acquiring the loss company.** A buyer acquires the loss corporation, or merges with it in a tax-free reorganization in which its losses carry over under Section 381. The losses survive, but subject to the Section 382 annual limit and the other rules described here.
- **Recapitalizing the loss company.** New capital is raised in amounts and forms designed to keep cumulative ownership shifts below the 50-point threshold, so the losses avoid a new Section 382 limit, though any existing limits and other rules continue to apply.
- **Restructuring in bankruptcy.** Two special rules apply in a Chapter 11 case. Under Section 382(l)(5), if pre-change shareholders and qualified creditors end up owning at least half of the reorganized company, the ordinary annual limit may not apply at all; in exchange, losses are reduced by certain interest deductions, and a second ownership change within two years eliminates the losses entirely. Under Section 382(l)(6), the limit still applies, but the company’s value is measured after the restructuring, reflecting the debt cancelled, which usually produces a larger limit.

Public companies with significant NOLs often adopt tax benefit preservation plans, which discourage stock purchases that could trigger an inadvertent ownership change.

### Illustrative example

*Assumptions:* a domestic C corporation holds \$50 million of NOLs arising after 2017; it has no earlier Section 382 limitation and no value adjustments; the federal rate is a constant 21 percent; and no other rule disallows the losses.

The nominal value of the losses is \$10.5 million of federal tax (\$50 million × 21 percent), an upper bound, not a market or accounting value.

If an investor acquires the company in an ownership change in November 2026, when its equity is worth \$10 million and the IRS’s long-term tax-exempt rate is 4.18 percent, Section 382 limits the use of the losses to \$418,000 a year, worth at most about \$87,800 of federal tax a year. At that pace the losses would take more than a century to use, so their economic value to the buyer is a small fraction of their nominal value.

If the same company instead acquires, without an ownership change, a profitable business that adds \$5 million a year of taxable income before the NOL deduction, it may be able to offset up to 80 percent of that income, or \$4 million a year, saving up to \$840,000 of federal tax annually, subject to Sections 269 and 384, consolidated return rules and the facts.

Illustrative and simplified. Rates change monthly, and the example ignores state tax, timing, the present value of future savings and many other rules that may apply.

### Limitations and risks

- **Section 269.** The IRS can disallow a deduction or credit where the principal purpose of an acquisition of control, or of property with a carryover basis, was the evasion or avoidance of federal income tax by securing a benefit the acquirer would not otherwise enjoy. A tax motive alone does not trigger the rule, but it must not be the principal purpose, and avoiding a Section 382 ownership change does not protect a transaction from it.
- **Economic substance.** Under Section 7701(o), a transaction is respected only if it meaningfully changes the taxpayer’s economic position apart from its tax effects and has a substantial non-tax purpose. A transaction that exists only to use a loss can be disregarded, and penalties can apply.
- **Section 384.** When one corporation acquires control of another, or its assets in a reorganization, and either has a net unrealized built-in gain, preacquisition losses of the other generally cannot offset that gain when it is recognized during the five-year recognition period. The rule reaches recognized built-in gains, not ordinary post-acquisition operating income, and its application depends on definitions and exceptions in the statute.
- **Business continuity.** Failing to continue the loss company’s business enterprise for two years after an ownership change generally reduces the Section 382 limit to zero.
- **Measurement.** Whether an ownership change has occurred depends on a detailed study of shareholder history, options and prior transactions. Errors are common and costly.
- **State law.** States set their own NOL rules, and conformity varies. California has suspended NOL deductions for tax years 2024 through 2026 for corporations with \$1 million or more of income subject to California tax and for individuals with \$1 million or more of net business income or modified adjusted gross income. Disaster loss carryovers are not affected, and the carryover period for suspended losses is extended by up to three years. Other states have imposed their own caps or suspensions from time to time.
- **Valuation.** Multiplying a loss by the tax rate gives an upper bound under simplified assumptions, not a market, accounting or realizable economic value. The real value depends on when the loss can actually be used under the applicable limits and on the likelihood of future taxable income.

### What about capital loss carryforwards?

A C corporation’s capital losses can offset only capital gains. Under Section 1212 they generally carry back three years and forward five, then expire, and after an ownership change they are limited under Section 383 alongside NOLs. Because of the short carryforward, timing a transaction that generates capital gains is often the central question.

Individuals are treated differently: net capital losses can offset capital gains plus up to \$3,000 of ordinary income a year (\$1,500 if married filing separately), and the remainder carries forward for life. As with NOLs, an individual’s capital loss carryforward cannot be transferred to another person and expires after the final return following death.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Can an individual sell or transfer an NOL?**

No. An individual’s NOL can be used only on that individual’s own returns, including a final joint return with a surviving spouse, and any unused amount expires after the final return following death.

**Q: Does a merger eliminate a company’s NOLs?**

Not necessarily. In many tax-free reorganizations the losses carry over to the surviving corporation under Section 381, but they become subject to the Section 382 annual limit if an ownership change has occurred, and to the other rules described in this article.

**Q: What is the long-term tax-exempt rate?**

A rate the IRS publishes each month for Section 382 purposes, equal to the highest of the adjusted federal long-term rates for the current month and the two prior months. The rate for the month of the ownership change is used to calculate the annual limit. For November 2026 it is 4.18 percent.

**Q: How much is an NOL worth?**

Its nominal value is the loss multiplied by the tax rate, which is an upper bound. Its economic value depends on how quickly it can be used under the applicable limits and on the likelihood of future taxable income, and is usually much lower.

## 2. Solo 401(k) vs. self-directed IRA, and when Roth makes sense

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/solo-401k-vs-self-directed-ira/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/solo-401k-vs-self-directed-ira
- **Category:** Retirement accounts (Retirement accounts · Tax strategy)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How a solo 401(k) and a self-directed IRA differ on contribution limits, investments, loans, debt-financed real estate and creditor protection, and the trade-offs of the Roth versions of each.

### The short answer

A solo 401(k) is a full employer retirement plan for a business owner with no employees other than a spouse. It allows far larger contributions than an IRA (up to \$72,000 in 2026 before catch-up contributions), permits loans, and avoids tax on debt-financed real estate. A self-directed IRA is open to anyone with earned income and can hold the same alternative investments, but contributions are capped at \$7,500 (\$8,600 at age 50 and over) and it offers no loans. The Roth version of either trades a tax deduction today for tax-free withdrawals later.

### What is a solo 401(k)?

A solo 401(k), also called a one-participant 401(k), is an ordinary 401(k) plan sponsored by a business whose only participants are the owner and the owner’s spouse. Sole proprietors, single-member LLCs, partnerships and S or C corporations can all sponsor one, so long as the business has no common-law employees who would have to be covered.

The owner contributes in two capacities. As the employee, the owner can defer up to \$24,500 of compensation in 2026, plus an \$8,000 catch-up contribution at age 50 or over, or \$11,250 at ages 60 through 63. As the employer, the business can add a profit-sharing contribution of up to 25 percent of W-2 compensation, or roughly 20 percent of net self-employment income for a sole proprietor. Total contributions are capped at \$72,000 for 2026, before catch-up contributions.

### What is a self-directed IRA?

A self-directed IRA is a traditional or Roth IRA held with a custodian that permits investments beyond publicly traded securities, such as real estate, private loans, private company shares and LLC interests. The tax rules are the same as for any IRA. What differs is the custodian, which allows the investment and holds title on the account’s behalf.

Contributions are limited to \$7,500 for 2026, plus a \$1,100 catch-up at age 50 or over. Anyone with earned income can contribute, but a deduction for a traditional IRA contribution phases out at higher incomes for people covered by a workplace plan, and Roth IRA contributions phase out between \$153,000 and \$168,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and between \$242,000 and \$252,000 for married couples filing jointly.

### How do they compare?

|                           | Solo 401(k)                                           | Self-directed IRA                                                                         |
|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Who can use it            | Business owners with no employees other than a spouse | Anyone with earned income                                                                 |
| 2026 contribution limit   | Up to \$72,000 combined, plus catch-up                | \$7,500, plus \$1,100 catch-up at 50+                                                     |
| Roth option               | Yes, with no income limit                             | Yes, subject to income limits (or a backdoor conversion)                                  |
| Loans                     | Permitted: the lesser of \$50,000 or half the balance | Not permitted                                                                             |
| Debt-financed real estate | Generally exempt from tax on the leveraged income     | Tax on unrelated debt-financed income applies                                             |
| Alternative investments   | Yes, if the plan document allows                      | Yes, through a self-directed custodian                                                    |
| Annual filing             | Form 5500-EZ once assets exceed \$250,000             | None by the owner; custodian reports                                                      |
| Bankruptcy protection     | Generally unlimited                                   | Capped (about \$1.7 million, adjusted periodically), with rollovers excluded from the cap |

### Pros and cons of each

#### Solo 401(k)

- **Pros.** Much higher contribution limits; a Roth option with no income ceiling; plan loans; the exemption for debt-financed real estate under Section 514(c)(9); and, because it is a trust the owner can administer, checkbook-style control without a custodian’s fees on each transaction.
- **Cons.** Available only while the business has no eligible employees; a plan document and annual Form 5500-EZ once assets pass \$250,000; the owner bears responsibility for administration; and creditor protection outside bankruptcy depends on state law, since a plan covering only an owner is not protected by ERISA’s anti-alienation rule in the same way.

#### Self-directed IRA

- **Pros.** Available to anyone with earned income, including employees; simple to open; can receive rollovers from employer plans; and can hold almost any investment other than life insurance and most collectibles.
- **Cons.** Low annual contributions; no loans; tax on income from leveraged investments; custodian fees on every transaction; and limited creditor protection in some states. In California, for example, IRAs are protected from judgment creditors only to the extent necessary for support.

### The rule that matters most: prohibited transactions

Both accounts are subject to the prohibited transaction rules of Section 4975. The account may not lend to, buy from, sell to or provide services to the owner, the owner’s spouse, ancestors or descendants, or businesses they control. The owner cannot live in, vacation at or personally manage a property the account owns, and cannot guarantee a loan to the account.

The consequences are severe. For an IRA, a prohibited transaction generally causes the entire account to be treated as distributed on the first day of that year, with income tax and possibly a 10 percent early-withdrawal penalty on the full balance. Self-directed accounts make these mistakes easier to commit, which is why they call for careful structuring.

### Roth or traditional?

A traditional contribution is deductible now and taxed when withdrawn. A Roth contribution is made after tax, and qualified withdrawals, including all growth, are tax-free. For alternative investments with high expected returns, that difference can be very large.

#### Pros of Roth

- Tax-free growth and tax-free qualified withdrawals, which matter most for assets expected to appreciate sharply.
- No required minimum distributions during the owner’s life, for Roth IRAs and, since 2024, for Roth accounts in 401(k) plans.
- Tax diversification: a pool of money that can be drawn without raising taxable income in retirement.
- Heirs inherit income-tax-free, although most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited account within ten years.

#### Cons of Roth

- No deduction today, which costs most when current tax rates are higher than expected future rates.
- Five-year holding rules apply before earnings can be withdrawn tax-free.
- Direct Roth IRA contributions are barred above the income limits. A backdoor Roth (a nondeductible IRA contribution converted to Roth) is available, but the pro-rata rule taxes part of the conversion if the owner holds other pre-tax IRA balances.
- A conversion of an existing traditional balance is fully taxable in the year it occurs.

### Which is right?

For a self-employed owner with no employees, the solo 401(k) is usually the stronger vehicle: it accepts far more money, offers a Roth option at any income, permits loans and treats leveraged real estate more favorably. A self-directed IRA suits people without a qualifying business, those consolidating rollovers, and investors who want a simple account for a few alternative holdings. Many owners use both.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Can I have a solo 401(k) and an IRA at the same time?**

Yes. IRA contributions are limited separately, though a solo 401(k) counts as a workplace plan for purposes of the traditional IRA deduction phase-out.

**Q: Can my solo 401(k) or IRA buy real estate?**

Yes, if the plan or custodian permits it. The property must be held purely as an investment, with all expenses paid by the account and all income returned to it, and neither the owner nor family members may use it.

**Q: What happens if I hire employees?**

Once the business has eligible employees, the plan must cover them under the usual 401(k) nondiscrimination rules, and a solo plan is generally converted or replaced.

**Q: Is a backdoor Roth allowed?**

Yes. Converting a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution to a Roth IRA is permitted, but other pre-tax IRA balances make part of the conversion taxable under the pro-rata rule. Rolling those balances into a solo 401(k) first can avoid that result.

## 3. Revocable vs. irrevocable trusts

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trusts/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/revocable-vs-irrevocable-trusts
- **Category:** Trusts and estate planning (Trusts · Estate planning)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** What each kind of trust does and does not do: probate, control, creditor protection, estate tax and income tax, and when families use one or both.

### The short answer

A revocable trust can be changed or cancelled at any time, so the person who creates it keeps full control. It avoids probate and provides for incapacity, but it offers no protection from creditors and no estate tax savings. An irrevocable trust generally cannot be changed once funded. In exchange for giving up control, it can remove assets and their future growth from the taxable estate and, when properly designed, protect them from creditors.

### What is a revocable trust?

A revocable trust, often called a living trust, holds assets during the creator’s lifetime under terms the creator can change at will. The creator is usually the trustee and the beneficiary while alive, names a successor trustee to take over at incapacity or death, and directs how the assets pass afterward.

For tax purposes a revocable trust is ignored. Income is reported on the creator’s own return, and the assets are included in the creator’s taxable estate under Section 2038. Because the assets are included, heirs generally receive a stepped-up basis at death under Section 1014.

### What is an irrevocable trust?

An irrevocable trust is one the creator cannot simply amend or revoke. The creator transfers assets to an independent or family trustee, who manages them for the beneficiaries under the trust’s terms. Depending on its design, the trust may be taxed as its own taxpayer or, as a grantor trust under Sections 671 through 679, have its income reported on the creator’s return even though the assets are outside the estate.

“Irrevocable” is less rigid than it sounds. Modern trusts often name a trust protector, allow decanting into a new trust, or grant limited powers of appointment, so that terms can adapt over decades without returning control to the creator.

### How do they compare?

|                                        | Revocable trust                     | Irrevocable trust                                          |
|----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Can be changed                         | Yes, at any time                    | Generally no, except through built-in flexibility          |
| Avoids probate                         | Yes, for assets titled in the trust | Yes                                                        |
| Creditor protection for the creator    | None                                | Yes, if properly designed and funded well before any claim |
| Removes assets from the taxable estate | No                                  | Yes, in most designs                                       |
| Step-up in basis at death              | Yes                                 | Generally no, unless the assets are included in the estate |
| Income tax                             | Reported by the creator             | By the creator (grantor trust) or by the trust itself      |
| Privacy                                | Not public, unlike a probate file   | Not public                                                 |

### Pros and cons of a revocable trust

- **Pros.** Avoids the cost, delay and publicity of probate, which can be considerable in states such as California; provides seamless management if the creator becomes incapacitated; keeps the stepped-up basis at death; and is simple to change as circumstances do.
- **Cons.** No protection from the creator’s creditors, since the creator can take the assets back at any time (California Probate Code section 18200 says so expressly); no estate tax savings; and it works only for assets actually retitled into it.

### Pros and cons of an irrevocable trust

- **Pros.** Removes assets and all future appreciation from the taxable estate; can protect assets from the creator’s and the beneficiaries’ creditors and from beneficiaries’ divorces; can hold life insurance outside the estate; and can last for several generations in states that allow long-duration trusts.
- **Cons.** Loss of direct control and access; assets generally do not receive a stepped-up basis at death (the IRS confirmed this for irrevocable grantor trusts in Revenue Ruling 2023-2); a non-grantor trust reaches the top income tax bracket at a small fraction of the income at which an individual does; and setup and ongoing trustee costs.

### Common types of irrevocable trust

- **Irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT).** Owns life insurance so the death benefit is outside the estate.
- **Spousal lifetime access trust (SLAT).** A gift to a trust for one spouse that the other spouse can benefit from indirectly.
- **Intentionally defective grantor trust (IDGT).** Outside the estate for estate tax purposes but a grantor trust for income tax purposes, often used for estate freezes.
- **Dynasty trust.** Holds assets for multiple generations outside each beneficiary’s estate.
- **Domestic asset protection trust (DAPT).** A self-settled trust, permitted in certain states, of which the creator can remain a discretionary beneficiary. See [the leading DAPT states compared](/knowledge/best-domestic-asset-protection-trust-states).
- **Charitable remainder and lead trusts.** Split interests between family and charity. See [charitable giving strategies](/knowledge/charitable-giving-strategies).

### Why the estate tax exemption matters

For 2026 the federal estate and gift tax exemption is \$15 million per person, \$30 million for a married couple, adjusted for inflation in later years. Families below that level often rely mainly on revocable trusts and value the step-up in basis more than estate exclusion. Families above it, or with assets likely to appreciate sharply, or with meaningful liability exposure, typically add irrevocable trusts. Several states impose their own estate taxes at much lower thresholds.

### Using both

Most comprehensive plans use both. A revocable trust serves as the hub that receives assets at death and avoids probate, while one or more irrevocable trusts hold the assets intended for estate tax savings, asset protection or long-term family stewardship.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Does a revocable trust protect assets from lawsuits?**

No. Because the creator can revoke it and take the assets back, the creator’s creditors can reach them during the creator’s lifetime.

**Q: Can an irrevocable trust ever be changed?**

Often, within limits. Depending on the trust terms and state law, a trust protector, decanting, nonjudicial settlement agreements or court modification can change administrative and sometimes dispositive terms.

**Q: Do assets in an irrevocable trust get a step-up in basis?**

Generally not, unless the trust is designed so the assets are included in the creator’s estate. Some trusts give an independent party a power to cause inclusion when the step-up would be worth more than the estate tax cost.

**Q: Who should be the trustee of an irrevocable trust?**

For tax and creditor protection, usually someone other than the creator, often an independent individual or trust company, sometimes alongside family members serving in advisory roles.

## 4. The five leading asset protection trust states compared

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/best-domestic-asset-protection-trust-states/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/best-domestic-asset-protection-trust-states
- **Category:** Asset protection (Asset protection · Trust jurisdictions)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** Nevada, South Dakota, Delaware, Alaska and Wyoming compared on waiting periods, exception creditors, taxes, trust duration and privacy, with the limits every domestic asset protection trust shares.

### The short answer

A domestic asset protection trust (DAPT) is an irrevocable trust that the creator can benefit from while shielding its assets from the creator’s future creditors. About twenty states allow them. Nevada and South Dakota are generally regarded as the strongest, with two-year waiting periods and no state income tax; Nevada is the only leading state with no exception creditors. Delaware and Alaska are long-established but use four-year periods and allow claims by certain spouses and preexisting creditors. Wyoming offers low cost and strong entity law.

### What is a domestic asset protection trust?

Traditionally, a person could not create a trust for their own benefit and keep it away from their own creditors. In 1997 Alaska changed that rule, and Delaware followed the same year. Today roughly twenty states permit a self-settled spendthrift trust, commonly called a domestic asset protection trust or DAPT.

The creator transfers assets to an irrevocable trust administered by a trustee in the DAPT state and remains eligible for discretionary distributions. After a statutory waiting period, creditors whose claims arise later generally cannot reach the trust, except for any categories of “exception creditors” the state allows.

### What to compare

- **Waiting period.** How long after a transfer a creditor can still challenge it.
- **Exception creditors.** Claimants, such as divorcing spouses, child support creditors or preexisting tort claimants, who can reach the trust regardless.
- **Burden of proof.** Whether a creditor must show fraudulent intent by clear and convincing evidence.
- **State income tax** on trust income, **trust duration**, **privacy** of court records and the depth of the local trustee market.

### The five leading states at a glance

|              | Waiting period                                               | Exception creditors                                                    | State income tax on trust                     | Notable feature                                                     |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nevada       | 2 years (or 6 months after discovery for existing creditors) | None                                                                   | None                                          | Exclusion of all exception creditors upheld in *Klabacka v. Nelson* |
| South Dakota | 2 years                                                      | Limited: child support, alimony and certain preexisting marital claims | None                                          | Perpetual trusts and sealed trust litigation records                |
| Delaware     | 4 years (or 1 year after discovery)                          | Divorcing spouses, child support, preexisting tort claimants           | Generally none for non-resident beneficiaries | Deep trust company market and Court of Chancery                     |
| Alaska       | 4 years (or 1 year after discovery)                          | Limited, including certain child support claims                        | None                                          | The first DAPT statute (1997)                                       |
| Wyoming      | 4 years for future creditors                                 | Child support and certain other claims                                 | None                                          | Low cost; strong LLC charging order law                             |

Statutes change and details vary with each state’s exceptions and procedures; confirm current law with counsel in the chosen state.

### Nevada

**Pros.** One of the shortest waiting periods; no exception creditors at all, which the Nevada Supreme Court enforced against spousal and child support claimants in *Klabacka v. Nelson* (2017); no state income tax; trusts can last 365 years; and strong charging order protection for LLCs.

**Cons.** Courts in other states may be less willing to honor Nevada law for a non-resident creator, and the absence of exception creditors draws scrutiny in divorce cases.

### South Dakota

**Pros.** Two-year waiting period; perpetual trusts; no state income tax; a statute that seals trust litigation records, offering unusual privacy; well-developed directed trust and trust protector law; and a large professional trustee industry.

**Cons.** Limited exception creditors for child support, alimony and certain claims by a spouse married before the transfer.

### Delaware

**Pros.** The most established trust infrastructure in the country, a specialized Court of Chancery, flexible directed trust law and generally no Delaware income tax on trust income accumulated for non-resident beneficiaries.

**Cons.** A four-year waiting period and a broad exception-creditor list, including divorcing spouses and preexisting tort claimants, make it weaker for pure creditor protection than Nevada or South Dakota.

### Alaska

**Pros.** The longest history of any DAPT state, no state income tax, perpetual trusts and a narrow set of exception creditors.

**Cons.** A four-year waiting period, and *Toni 1 Trust v. Wacker* (2018), in which the Alaska Supreme Court held that Alaska cannot force other states’ courts to leave fraudulent transfer claims to Alaska courts alone.

### Wyoming

**Pros.** Lower formation and trustee costs, no state income tax, trusts lasting up to 1,000 years, and some of the strongest LLC and charging order statutes in the country, which pair well with a trust.

**Cons.** A longer waiting period than Nevada or South Dakota and fewer decided cases testing its trust statute.

### Limits every DAPT shares

- **Residence of the creator.** A creator who lives in a state without a DAPT statute, such as California, faces the risk that a home-state court applies its own law. In *In re Huber* (2013), a Washington resident’s Alaska trust was set aside.
- **Bankruptcy.** Section 548(e) of the Bankruptcy Code lets a trustee recover transfers to a self-settled trust made within ten years with intent to hinder, delay or defraud creditors.
- **Full faith and credit.** A judgment from another state’s court can be enforced against assets in the DAPT state, so assets held in the creator’s home state are the most exposed.
- **Timing.** No DAPT protects against a claim that exists or is foreseeable when the trust is funded.

For residents of non-DAPT states, an offshore trust or a hybrid design is often considered. See [offshore asset protection jurisdictions compared](/knowledge/offshore-asset-protection-trust-jurisdictions).

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Do I have to live in Nevada or South Dakota to use its DAPT?**

No. Non-residents can create one by using a trustee in that state and keeping some administration there, but protection is strongest when the creator, the trustee and the assets all have ties to the DAPT state.

**Q: Can I be the trustee of my own DAPT?**

No. The statutes require at least one trustee in the state, typically a trust company or resident individual. The creator can often keep investment authority or a veto through a directed trust structure.

**Q: Is a DAPT a grantor trust for income tax?**

Usually yes, so the creator reports the income. It can also be designed as a completed gift that removes assets from the estate, or as an incomplete gift that keeps them in.

**Q: Which state is best?**

For most people focused on creditor protection, Nevada or South Dakota. The right answer depends on residence, the assets involved, family circumstances and whether estate tax planning is also a goal.

## 5. Offshore asset protection trusts: Cook Islands, Nevis, Belize, Cayman, Gibraltar and others compared

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/offshore-asset-protection-trust-jurisdictions/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/offshore-asset-protection-trust-jurisdictions
- **Category:** Offshore planning (Asset protection · Offshore jurisdictions)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How the leading offshore trust jurisdictions compare on limitation periods, burden of proof, recognition of U.S. judgments, cost and track record, and the U.S. reporting every offshore trust requires.

### The short answer

The Cook Islands and Nevis are the two jurisdictions most often used by U.S. families for offshore asset protection trusts. Both refuse to enforce U.S. judgments against trust assets, require a creditor to prove fraudulent intent beyond a reasonable doubt, and allow only a short window to challenge a transfer; Nevis also requires a creditor to post a bond of about US\$100,000 before suing. Belize offers immediate protection on paper but has less track record. The Cayman Islands, Gibraltar, the Bahamas and the Channel Islands are strong financial centers but weaker choices when creditor protection is the main goal. Every offshore trust remains fully reportable to the IRS.

### Why consider an offshore trust?

A domestic asset protection trust depends on U.S. courts honoring another state’s law. An offshore trust removes that dependency. Its trustee is outside U.S. jurisdiction, and the leading jurisdictions will not enforce a U.S. judgment against trust assets, so a creditor must start a new case in the foreign court under that country’s rules. That is why offshore trusts are generally regarded as among the strongest forms of asset protection available to U.S. persons, though their effectiveness depends on the jurisdiction, the timing of funding and the facts, and why they are commonly used by residents of states, such as California, that do not recognize self-settled trusts.

### How an offshore trust is typically structured

- The creator establishes an irrevocable trust with a licensed trustee in the chosen jurisdiction and remains a discretionary beneficiary.
- The trust usually owns an LLC, often one the creator manages while no threat exists, which holds investment accounts at U.S. institutions. Assets need not leave the United States day to day.
- If a serious claim arises, the creator is removed as manager, the foreign trustee takes control, and assets can be moved offshore under a “duress” clause that bars the trustee from following instructions given under court compulsion.
- A trust protector, often independent, can replace the trustee or change the governing jurisdiction.

### The jurisdictions at a glance

|                | Window to challenge a transfer   | Creditor’s burden                               | Enforces U.S. judgments   | Notable feature                                                     |
|----------------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|---------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Cook Islands   | 1 to 2 years                     | Beyond a reasonable doubt                       | No                        | Longest track record (since 1984)                                   |
| Nevis          | 1 to 2 years                     | Beyond a reasonable doubt                       | No                        | Creditor must post about US\$100,000 bond                           |
| Belize         | None stated in the trust statute | No express standard                             | No                        | Immediate protection on paper; limited case law                     |
| Cayman Islands | 6 years                          | Intent to defraud and transfer at an undervalue | In limited cases          | World-class financial center; not built for self-settled protection |
| Bahamas        | Short statutory period           | Intent to defraud                               | In limited cases          | Strong trust industry; self-settled protection less certain         |
| Gibraltar      | Varies                           | Intent to defraud                               | Under English-based rules | Well regulated; narrower asset protection features                  |

Summary only. Limitation periods in particular depend on when the creditor’s claim arose relative to the transfer; confirm current law with counsel in the jurisdiction.

### Cook Islands

The Cook Islands enacted its International Trusts Act in 1984, effectively creating the modern asset protection trust. A creditor must bring a claim in the Cook Islands High Court, generally within two years of the cause of action arising and within a short time after the transfer, and must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the transfer was intended to defraud that particular creditor.

**Pros.** Four decades of legislation and case law; the highest burden of proof; no recognition of foreign judgments; and an experienced, regulated trustee industry. Creditors rarely pursue claims there because of the cost and difficulty.

**Cons.** Higher setup and annual costs than most alternatives; and the trust cannot protect a creator from U.S. court orders directed at the creator personally (see contempt risk below).

### Nevis

Nevis’s International Exempt Trust Ordinance, enacted in 1994, closely follows the Cook Islands model, with the same beyond-reasonable-doubt standard and a short limitation period. Its distinctive feature is a requirement that a creditor deposit a bond of EC\$270,000, about US\$100,000, before bringing an action against a Nevis trust.

**Pros.** The bond requirement is a strong practical deterrent; Nevis LLCs are well regarded and often paired with the trust; and costs are generally below the Cook Islands.

**Cons.** A smaller body of decided cases than the Cook Islands and a smaller trustee market.

### Belize

Belize’s Trusts Act protects properly transferred trust assets from foreign fraudulent transfer claims without a stated waiting period, which is marketed as immediate protection.

**Pros.** No limitation period to wait out; low cost; flexible trust law.

**Cons.** Limited litigation history, a smaller and less established trustee industry, and concerns about regulatory stability. Many advisers prefer not to hold bank accounts there.

### Cayman Islands

The Cayman Islands is one of the world’s leading financial centers, with sophisticated trust law, including STAR trusts for purpose and commercial use. Its Fraudulent Dispositions Act allows a creditor to set aside a transfer made at an undervalue with intent to defraud, within six years.

**Pros.** Reputation, institutional depth and English-based courts; excellent for fund structures and multigenerational trusts.

**Cons.** A six-year challenge window and no express statutory framework for self-settled asset protection trusts make it a weaker choice when creditor protection is the primary goal.

### Gibraltar

Gibraltar, a British Overseas Territory, has a well-regulated financial sector and English-based trust law, and historically offered registered asset protection trusts under its insolvency legislation.

**Pros.** Strong regulation and reputation, proximity to Europe, and suitability for families with U.K. or European connections.

**Cons.** Its protections are generally narrower and less tested for U.S. creditors than those of the Cook Islands or Nevis, and it is seldom the first choice for U.S. asset protection.

### Other jurisdictions

- **Bahamas.** A large trust and banking industry and a short fraudulent disposition period, but less certainty for self-settled protection.
- **Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man.** Premier trust centers for multigenerational wealth and institutional trusteeship, chosen more for stability and administration than for aggressive creditor protection.
- **Liechtenstein and Switzerland.** Foundations and trusts with strong privacy traditions and civil law courts, used primarily by European families.

### U.S. tax and reporting

An offshore asset protection trust is not a tax shelter. It is almost always a grantor trust, so all income is reported on the creator’s U.S. return as if the trust did not exist. The creator must also file Form 3520 (for transfers and distributions) and ensure the trust files Form 3520-A each year, report foreign accounts on the FBAR, and report foreign financial assets on Form 8938 where thresholds are met. Penalties for missed filings are steep, often a percentage of the amount transferred.

### Limits and risks

- **Contempt of court.** A U.S. court can order the creator to repatriate assets. In *FTC v. Affordable Media* (1999), settlors who claimed they could not comply because of their trust’s duress clause were held in contempt and jailed. A creator who retains control is at greatest risk.
- **Timing.** Funding a trust after a claim arises can be a fraudulent transfer under U.S. law, and in bankruptcy Section 548(e) reaches transfers to self-settled trusts made within ten years with intent to defraud.
- **Assets in the United States.** Real estate and accounts that remain in the U.S. can be reached by U.S. courts. Protection depends on the trustee’s ability to move liquid assets offshore when needed.
- **Cost.** Setup and annual trustee, legal and reporting costs are significant, which generally makes offshore trusts appropriate only for substantial liquid wealth.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is an offshore asset protection trust legal?**

Yes, when it is funded with legitimately owned assets, before any claim exists or is foreseeable, and all U.S. tax and reporting obligations are met.

**Q: Do the assets have to leave the United States?**

Not initially. Many trusts hold U.S. accounts through an LLC. Protection is strongest when assets can be moved offshore if a serious threat arises.

**Q: Cook Islands or Nevis?**

Both are first-tier. The Cook Islands has the longer track record; Nevis adds the creditor bond and is usually less expensive. Many plans pair a Cook Islands trust with a Nevis or Cook Islands LLC.

**Q: Can an offshore trust protect U.S. real estate?**

Only indirectly. Real estate stays subject to U.S. courts, so it is usually protected through entities, equity stripping or domestic planning rather than by offshore ownership alone. See [equity stripping](/knowledge/equity-stripping).

## 6. Equity stripping: how it works and where it fails

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/equity-stripping/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/equity-stripping
- **Category:** Asset protection (Asset protection · Real estate)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** How equity stripping uses real, documented debt to reduce the equity a creditor can reach, the forms it takes, where the borrowed funds go, and the mistakes that let courts disregard it.

### The short answer

Equity stripping reduces the equity in an asset, usually real estate or a business, by placing legitimate, recorded debt against it. A creditor who obtains a judgment can only reach what is left after the lienholder is paid, so a heavily encumbered asset is a poor target. It works only when the debt is real: a genuine lender, real money advanced, commercial terms and actual payments. Friendly liens that exist only on paper are routinely set aside, and the borrowed money must itself be placed somewhere protected.

### What is equity stripping?

A judgment creditor collects by forcing the sale of a debtor’s assets. Any lender with a recorded lien on an asset is paid first, so the creditor can only reach the debtor’s equity: the value left after secured debt. Equity stripping deliberately reduces that equity by borrowing against the asset.

A property worth \$2 million with no mortgage offers a creditor \$2 million. The same property with \$1.6 million of recorded debt offers only \$400,000, less the cost of a forced sale, and may not be worth pursuing at all. The protection comes not from hiding the asset but from changing the economics of collecting against it.

### The main forms

- **Commercial financing.** A conventional mortgage, line of credit or business loan from an unrelated bank or private lender. This is the strongest form because the lender is plainly independent.
- **Affiliated or captive lending.** A loan from an entity the owner’s family or trust controls, sometimes funded by the owner’s other assets. It can work, but it invites scrutiny and must meet every formality of an arm’s-length loan.
- **Cross-collateralization.** Several properties or entities pledge assets for one another’s obligations, so equity in each is committed to real debts.
- **Business assets.** Receivables, equipment and intellectual property can be pledged through UCC Article 9 security interests, with filed financing statements establishing the lender’s priority.

### Where the borrowed money goes

Borrowing alone does not protect anything; it converts equity in the property into cash, and cash in the owner’s name is easy for a creditor to reach. The proceeds must go somewhere protected or productive, for example:

- an asset protection trust, domestic or offshore;
- assets exempt under state law, which can include cash-value life insurance and annuities in some states;
- investments in separate entities with strong charging order protection; or
- a genuine business use, such as acquiring other property.

The investment of the proceeds also determines whether the interest is deductible, because the tax rules generally trace interest to the use of the borrowed funds rather than to the property securing the loan.

### What makes it hold up

- **A real lender and real money.** Funds are actually advanced, and the debt is not merely acknowledged on paper.
- **Commercial terms.** Market interest rate, a stated maturity and the remedies a third-party lender would demand.
- **Actual performance.** Payments are made on schedule, and defaults are treated as a real lender would treat them.
- **Proper recording and perfection.** Deeds of trust are recorded and security interests perfected before any claim arises, so the lien has priority.
- **A legitimate purpose.** The financing fits a broader business or estate plan rather than appearing the week a lawsuit is filed.

### Where it fails

- **Sham or friendly liens.** A note to a relative with no money advanced, no payments and no intention of enforcement can be disregarded, and recording one after a claim arises may be a voidable transfer.
- **Timing.** Under the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act, adopted in California and most states, transfers and obligations incurred with intent to hinder, delay or defraud a creditor can be undone. Equity stripping done after a claim arises is especially vulnerable.
- **Unprotected proceeds.** If the loan proceeds sit in a personal account, the creditor simply collects the cash instead of the property.
- **Bankruptcy review.** A bankruptcy trustee can examine transfers and obligations made within the look-back periods under Section 548 and state law.

### Pros and cons

- **Pros.** Protects assets that cannot easily be retitled or moved, such as a home or an operating property; uses well-understood lending law; can be combined with trusts and entities; and, with an independent lender, is difficult to attack.
- **Cons.** Real debt carries real cost and real risk: interest expense, refinancing and maturity risk, and the possibility of foreclosure if values fall or payments stop. Existing loans may restrict further encumbrance, and the plan requires ongoing discipline to remain credible.

### Illustrative example

A physician owns an unencumbered rental building worth \$3 million. Years before any claim, she obtains a \$2.2 million commercial loan from an unrelated lender secured by the building and contributes the proceeds to an asset protection trust that invests them. A later malpractice judgment in excess of insurance can reach, at most, the building’s remaining equity after the lender is paid, and the invested proceeds sit in a structure designed to resist collection.

Illustrative only. The result depends on state law, timing, the trust’s design and the facts.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is equity stripping legal?**

Yes, when it involves genuine debt incurred for legitimate reasons before any claim exists. Arrangements designed to defeat a known or expected creditor can be set aside.

**Q: Can I borrow from my own company or trust?**

Sometimes, but related-party loans receive the closest scrutiny. They must be documented, funded, priced and serviced exactly as an independent loan would be.

**Q: Does equity stripping work on a primary residence?**

It can, alongside the state’s homestead exemption. Interest on debt not used to buy or improve the home may not be deductible, depending on how the proceeds are used.

**Q: What happens if property values fall?**

The owner remains liable for the debt. Equity stripping should leave a margin of safety and be sized to cash flow the owner can sustain.

## 7. Charitable giving strategies compared: from donor-advised funds to NIMCRUTs

- **Staging URL:** https://orellestudio.com/paratum/knowledge/charitable-giving-strategies/
- **Production URL (planned):** https://paratum.com/knowledge/charitable-giving-strategies
- **Category:** Tax strategy (Tax strategy · Philanthropy)
- **Date:** 2026-11-23 · **Author:** Paratum · **Reviewer:** Aaron Sokol, JD, MBA (review pending)
- **Summary:** Outright gifts, appreciated stock, donor-advised funds, private foundations, charitable remainder trusts including the NIMCRUT and flip unitrust, lead trusts, gift annuities and IRA gifts, with the 2026 rule changes.

### The short answer

The simplest charitable strategy is a gift of appreciated stock, which avoids capital gains tax and earns a deduction for full market value. Donor-advised funds add timing flexibility; private foundations add control at higher cost. Charitable remainder trusts pay the donor an income stream, defer tax on the sale of appreciated assets and leave the remainder to charity; the NIMCRUT variant lets payments be deferred and made up later, which can turn it into a supplemental retirement vehicle. Charitable lead trusts do the reverse, paying charity first and passing the remainder to family. From 2026, new floors and caps reduce the deduction for many itemizers, which makes structure matter more.

### What changed in 2026

The 2025 tax law made several changes that take effect in 2026. Itemized charitable deductions now count only to the extent they exceed 0.5 percent of adjusted gross income. For taxpayers in the top bracket, the value of itemized deductions is capped at a 35 percent benefit. Taxpayers who take the standard deduction can deduct up to \$1,000 of cash gifts (\$2,000 for joint filers). The 60 percent of AGI limit on cash gifts to public charities is now permanent, and the federal estate and gift tax exemption is \$15 million per person.

The effect is to reward larger, planned gifts over small annual ones, and to make structures that avoid capital gains tax, or that work outside the income tax deduction entirely, relatively more valuable.

### Outright gifts of cash and appreciated assets

A cash gift to a public charity is deductible up to 60 percent of AGI. A gift of stock or other property held more than a year is generally deductible at full market value, up to 30 percent of AGI, and neither the donor nor the charity pays tax on the built-in gain. Excess deductions carry forward five years.

**Pros.** Simple; for appreciated assets, a double benefit. **Cons.** No retained income or control; gifts of real estate, private stock and other illiquid assets require qualified appraisals and a charity willing to accept them.

### Donor-advised funds

A donor-advised fund is an account at a sponsoring public charity. The donor takes the deduction when assets are contributed and recommends grants to charities over time.

**Pros.** Immediate deduction with deferred grant-making, useful for “bunching” several years of giving into one high-income year; accepts appreciated and some illiquid assets; low cost; public-charity deduction limits. **Cons.** The sponsor has legal control of the assets; grants cannot provide benefits to the donor; and IRA qualified charitable distributions cannot be made to a donor-advised fund.

### Private foundations

A private foundation is a separate charitable entity the family controls, typically with family members as directors.

**Pros.** Permanent family control over investments and grants; a vehicle for family governance and legacy; can employ family members at reasonable compensation. **Cons.** Lower deduction limits (generally 30 percent of AGI for cash and 20 percent for appreciated property, with most non-publicly traded property deductible only at cost basis); an excise tax on net investment income; a required minimum distribution of about 5 percent of assets each year; strict self-dealing rules; and annual public filings.

### Charitable remainder trusts

A charitable remainder trust (CRT) pays the donor, or other individuals, an annual amount for life or for a term of up to 20 years, and gives what remains to charity. The trust itself is tax-exempt, so it can sell appreciated property without immediate capital gains tax and reinvest the full proceeds. The donor receives a deduction for the present value of the charity’s remainder, which must be at least 10 percent of the amount contributed. The annual payout must be between 5 and 50 percent.

- **Charitable remainder annuity trust (CRAT).** Pays a fixed dollar amount each year. Predictable, but no additional contributions are allowed, and it must pass a test showing a low probability that the trust will be exhausted.
- **Standard charitable remainder unitrust (CRUT).** Pays a fixed percentage of the trust’s value, revalued each year, so payments rise and fall with the portfolio. Additional contributions are allowed.
- **Net income unitrust (NICRUT).** Pays the lesser of the stated percentage or the trust’s actual income. Shortfalls are not made up.
- **Net income with makeup unitrust (NIMCRUT).** Pays the lesser of the percentage or actual income, but records any shortfall in a makeup account that can be paid in later years when income exceeds the stated percentage.
- **Flip unitrust.** Begins as a net income trust and converts to a standard unitrust after a permitted triggering event, such as the sale of real estate or another hard-to-sell asset, or a date or life event specified in the trust.

Payments to the beneficiary are taxed under a tiered system: ordinary income first, then capital gains, then tax-exempt income, then a return of principal. A CRT therefore defers tax rather than eliminating it.

### The NIMCRUT in more detail

Because a NIMCRUT pays only what the trust earns as “income” under its terms and state law, the trustee can control the timing of payments through the choice of investments. A trust invested for growth, or in an investment that produces no current distributable income, pays little in the early years while the makeup account grows. When the donor wants income, typically in retirement, the trustee shifts to income-producing investments and the trust can pay the current amount plus the accumulated makeup.

**Common uses.** A supplemental retirement plan for business owners and professionals who have already maximized qualified plans; deferring income from the sale of a business or highly appreciated property; and, in a flip design, holding real estate or private stock until it sells and then converting to a regular payout.

**Pros.** Tax-deferred growth and reinvestment of the full sale proceeds; flexibility over when income is received; a current deduction; and a charitable legacy.

**Cons.** Irrevocable, with the remainder committed to charity; payments depend on the trust’s definition of income and on investment choices, which require careful drafting and an independent trustee; the makeup account is never guaranteed if the trust declines in value; and the IRS has historically scrutinized aggressive income-timing arrangements, so the structure must be conservative and properly administered.

### Charitable lead trusts

A charitable lead trust reverses the CRT: charity receives payments for a term, and the remainder passes to family. In a non-grantor lead trust, the gift to family is valued at a steep discount, and in a “zeroed-out” design it can be close to zero, so growth above the IRS’s Section 7520 rate passes to heirs free of gift tax. A grantor lead trust instead gives the donor an upfront income tax deduction, with the trust’s income taxed to the donor afterward.

**Pros.** Transfers appreciation to the next generation at low transfer-tax cost while supporting charity. **Cons.** Works best when interest rates are low and returns high; heirs wait for the lead term to end; and the arithmetic must be modeled carefully.

### Charitable gift annuities and pooled income funds

A charitable gift annuity is a contract under which a charity pays a fixed lifetime annuity in exchange for a gift, with part of the gift deductible. It is simple and inexpensive but is backed only by the charity’s general credit. Pooled income funds, maintained by some larger charities, combine many donors’ gifts and pay each a share of the fund’s income.

### Qualified charitable distributions from IRAs

Individuals aged 70½ or older can transfer up to \$111,000 in 2026 directly from an IRA to public charities. The distribution is excluded from income rather than deducted, so it bypasses the new 0.5 percent floor and 35 percent cap, and it counts toward required minimum distributions. It cannot be made to a donor-advised fund or private foundation.

### Frequently asked questions

**Q: Does a charitable remainder trust eliminate capital gains tax?**

No. It defers it. The trust sells without immediate tax, but distributions carry out the gain to the beneficiary over time under the tiered taxation rules.

**Q: Can I be the trustee of my own charitable remainder trust?**

Often yes, but for a NIMCRUT or a trust holding hard-to-value assets, an independent trustee or special trustee is usually advisable to avoid self-dealing and valuation problems.

**Q: What is the 10 percent remainder test?**

The present value of the charity’s remainder interest, calculated using IRS tables, must be at least 10 percent of the value contributed. Young beneficiaries, long terms and high payouts can cause a trust to fail it.

**Q: Is a donor-advised fund or a private foundation better?**

A donor-advised fund is simpler, cheaper and offers higher deduction limits. A private foundation offers control and permanence at greater cost and regulation. Some families use both.
